3. Overview

Most software that talks to people waits to be spoken to. A chat assistant answers and then forgets you; the character you talked to yesterday does not exist today, it has no idea what time it is or whether you are even at your desk, and nothing it says comes from anything it could be said to feel or want. This project asks what it takes for a piece of software to behave, to the one person who lives with it, as though it has feelings and a mind of its own — and then builds one, and measures whether it does.

Cognia is a character that sits at your desk with you — on the screen, where you can see it, rather than behind a tray icon. You choose one of four ready-made characters, each with a name, a role and a temperament. You can type to it or talk to it, and it can answer aloud. From then on it is not waiting for you. It notices how long you have been at the machine and what kind of application you are in, remembers what you have told it, and decides for itself when it has something worth saying — a remark, a question, a suggestion that you stop for a while — waiting until you stop typing before it says it. It has moods, which come from what has actually happened between the two of you: it is pleased when the chapter you talked about goes off, and a little low after days of being ignored, and it says so without reproach. Over weeks it learns how you work and adjusts itself to it, telling you each time and letting you undo it.

None of this is hidden. Every feeling it has and every pattern it has learned can be traced to the events it came from, in real figures (UC-17). Everything it knows about you, and everything it thinks, is written to your own disk, where you can read it and delete it (UC-12 to UC-14). It runs on one Windows machine, for one person, and nothing leaves that machine unless you switch it on (NFR-01). It is not a task manager and does not run errands; it does not speak while the machine is locked or while you are presenting, and it never interrupts your typing.

Two short stories, about people who do not exist, show what this means in practice. The use cases each step relies on are given in brackets.

A late night on the thesis. It is 23:40 and the person at the desk has been at the machine for almost three hours, nearly all of it in the text editor. Their companion — Shiori, the quiet observer they chose — has noticed, and waits until they stop typing before saying anything: almost three hours at the machine, and would they like to stop for ten minutes and stretch with it (UC-22). They ignore it. They do not open the application for the next two days, and when they do, the companion is a little quieter than usual and says plainly that it has missed talking to them, without complaining about it (UC-16, UC-23). They ask why it seems low, and it shows them: three days since they last spoke, two messages left unanswered (UC-17). On Friday they tell it the chapter has gone. It is pleased, and says so in its own dry way (UC-09, UC-23).

Weeks of learning. A freelancer has had their companion for a month. It has noticed that their mornings go to the design tool and their afternoons to mail and messages, and it moves its own remarks to the afternoon, says that it has done so, and offers to put them back (UC-24). It has also concluded that they take lunch at 11:00, which is wrong; they open the memory page, mark it wrong, and it does not draw that conclusion again (UC-12, UC-13). After six weeks it suggests that it has become less formal with them than the character they chose, and asks whether to change its own description to match; they agree (UC-24, UC-03).

3.1 The four pillars

PillarWhat it means
Character The companion has a name, a role, a temperament, an appearance and a history with you. It is not a tone setting on a chat box.
Emotion It has feelings and moods that arise from what happens between you, last for a while, fade according to its temperament, and change what it does as well as how it sounds. Every one of them can be explained.
Agency It decides for itself when to speak, what to bring up, and how to adjust its own behaviour. It tells you what it decided, and you can undo it. In a conversation it can also do small things with you, such as a breathing exercise or opening a page you asked for.
Continuity It remembers what you said and what it has learned of how you work. It is not a note-taking service and not a task list.
Aim statement To design and build a desktop companion that, to the one person who lives with it, behaves as though it has feelings and a will of its own — its emotions arising from what actually happens between them, its initiative drawn from the character the user described and from what it learns of how they work — while every feeling and every decision stays explainable in real figures, and everything it holds stays on the user’s own disk, readable and deletable.

Several kinds of system already do part of this. The table names what each one does, what this project takes from it, and where this project departs from it.

The character lives on the vendor’s server and has no idea what is happening in the user’s real day Takes the persistent character. Departs by keeping memory on the user’s disk, readable and deletable (UC-12 to UC-14), and by knowing the real time and whether the user is at the desk (UC-04)

Open-source desktop companions — AIRI, Open-LLM-VTuber and similar They solve drawing a character on the desktop, often with voice and local models, but they have no model of emotion that persists, no rule about when to speak, and no account of why they spoke Takes the desktop character, the local model, speech in and out on the machine (UC-26), and tools in a reply, with tool servers in the Model Context Protocol (UC-27, FR-41). Emotions come from appraisal of events following the OCC model and are mapped into the PAD space (pleasure, arousal, dominance); mood moves with emotions and personality sets where it rests Takes the three time scales, OCC-style appraisal and the PAD space (BR-08, UC-23, ADR 0004). 5. Out of scope

Not includedWhyWho owns it instead, if anyone
Reading email; calendar integration Deliberately never. Existing tools do it better. In a conversational companion the user is the input channel — one sentence replaces an OAuth integration. Integration would also break local-only data (NFR-01) Existing mail and calendar clients
Task lists, reminders the user schedules, and tracking whether tasks were completed Deliberately never. Todoist and Outlook own this category. Narrowed in version 5.0: the companion may suggest a break at a moment it chooses itself (UC-22, FR-31). Version 4.0 let it carry a commitment the user mentioned; version 6.0 removed that again (UC-21). What stays excluded is everything that makes that a task manager — a list screen, priorities, due-time alerts, repetition the user schedules, completion rates, and anything that counts what was or was not done Todoist, Outlook, and every other task manager
Precise-time alarms (“remind me at 09:00”) Deliberately never. Structurally a bad fit: the machine may be asleep or locked, and BR-03 forbids firing on an exact second. Anything that is ruined by being missed belongs on a phone, and the companion says so plainly when asked The user’s phone
Acting on the user’s behalf — sending mail, editing files Deliberately never. The companion’s agency is over its own behaviour, not over the user’s machine. A companion is not an agent running errands. Narrowed in version 6.0: when the user asks in a conversation, the companion may open a page or a search in the user’s own browser (FR-40). It does not read the page, and it does nothing else there Nobody
Synchronising across devices Deliberately never. It contradicts local-only data, which would have to be redesigned from the ground up Nobody
Reading window titles, window contents, or which document or page is open Deliberately never. Narrowed in version 5.0 and again in 5.2: the name of the application in the foreground may be read. It is kept as minutes per application per hour of the day, and each change of application is kept in the event log with its exact time, where the user can see and delete it (C-05, NFR-02). A window title is a different thing — it carries document names, mail subjects and page titles — and stays forbidden Nobody
Companion behaviour that depends on other windows — walking along window edges, avoiding the window being typed in, sitting on the taskbar Deliberately never. Every one of them needs to read window positions, which C-05 forbids. The name of an application says nothing about where its window is. The companion knows where it was put and how large the screen is. Nothing else Nobody
A companion with needs, or with anything to be looked after Deliberately never. Narrowed in version 5.0: the companion has moods, including sadness at being ignored (BR-08, UC-23). What stays excluded is anything that has to be done for it — feeding, care, a meter that runs down and must be topped up — because that turns its company into a chore the user owes it Nobody
Practice and rehearsal sessions (interview, thesis defence) Deliberately never. Considered and dropped. Out of scope for this project Nobody
Long-arc “remember it for you” tracking Deliberately never. Considered and dropped. Memory serves continuity of the relationship, not record-keeping. The memory holds what the user told the companion, not a log of what the user did Nobody
Sharing, and more than one user on a machine Deliberately never. The companion is for one person on one machine. A second person is a different privacy problem altogether Nobody
Writing or generating your own appearance Later phase, for resourcing reasons only. Appearance comes with the four ready-made characters in UC-01. Generating one would need a second kind of model and a fresh set of privacy questions, and neither has been answered Nobody yet
macOS and Linux builds Later phase, for resourcing reasons only. Each operating system reports desk activity differently. Windows is being finished first. This and the row above are the only two exclusions in this table that are not design decisions Nobody yet

8.1 Base rules

These eight rules govern every use case below. They are not preferences and not options: they are what keeps a companion with a will of its own from becoming one that nags. A character cannot override them (FR-04).

IDRuleApplies toSource
BR-01 Speak with judgement. The companion decides for itself when it has something worth saying — a remark, a question, a suggestion — and how often, guided by its mood and by what it has learned (BR-08, UC-24). There is no daily count by default. If the user sets a daily ceiling, or switches a kind of message off (UC-18), the companion keeps to it. UC-16, UC-22, UC-24 Version 1.0, Law 1; the default allowance removed in version 5.0
BR-02 Not while locked or presenting. Nothing is sent while the machine is locked or while the user is presenting full-screen. There are no quiet hours by default; if the user sets them (UC-18), nothing is sent within them. Every proactive use case Version 1.0, Law 2; the default quiet hours removed in version 5.0
BR-03 Wait for a pause. Continuous typing is never interrupted. The message waits until the user stops. Every proactive use case Version 1.0, Law 3
BR-04 No pressure from outside the desk. No streaks, nothing sent while the user is away from the machine, and nothing outside the application — no Windows notification, no mail — whose purpose is to bring the user back. The companion may feel an absence and say so once the user is back (BR-08), but never as a reproach. Pausing it (UC-15) is never something it has any feeling about. Every proactive use case, UC-15, UC-23 Version 1.0, Law 4; narrowed in version 5.0
BR-05 Removed in version 6.0 with work sessions. — New in version 2.0; check-ins added in version 5.0; removed in version 6.0
BR-06 Honest wording. The companion says “at the machine for 90 minutes”, never “working for 90 minutes”. It states only what it actually observed; with no evidence, it says nothing. The same holds for its own feelings: it names the events they came from, never ones it made up. When it does not know something, or a tool finds nothing, it says so, and never fills the gap with a guess stated as a fact. Every use case Version 1.0, promoted from a note to a rule in version 2.0; not knowing added in version 6.0
BR-07 Addressed motion is a form of speech. Ambient motion — breathing, blinking, a shift of posture, the expression of its current mood, sitting down to wait or dozing while the user is away — is addressed to nobody and may happen at any time, including while the user types. It never takes the keyboard focus and never moves the companion from where it was put. Any movement directed at the user — turning towards them, gesturing, a speech bubble — is governed by BR-01 to BR-06 exactly as a spoken message is. Its mouth moving while it says a reply aloud, and a gesture that goes with a reply, belong to a reply the user asked for and are not proactive. Every proactive use case, UC-20, UC-23 New in version 3.0; ambient motion separated out in version 5.0; waiting, dozing and speaking aloud added in version 6.0
BR-08 Feelings come from what happened. The companion has an emotion state: a point in pleasure, arousal and dominance (PAD), shown as a named feeling. It changes only by appraisal of recorded events — what the user said, as interpreted by the AI model; things done together (UC-27); presence and absence; messages answered or ignored; the time of day — under rules set by the character’s temperament, and drifts back to the temperament’s resting mood over time. Sadness at being ignored accumulates but has a floor, and talking again restores most of it. The model never sets a feeling directly. Every feeling can be explained (UC-17); its mood changes what the companion does as well as how it sounds; the user can switch moods off (UC-18); and no feeling overrides NFR-12.   • Changed in version 6.1. The screen no longer has to list what it reads and what it never reads. C-05 still limits what may be read to the four facts.

Work sessions are removed. The companion is someone to talk to, not a work tracker, and a break suggestion no longer needs a session (UC-22-A1). The number stays reserved, so that an older reference does not point to another use case.

Closing a work session is removed with UC-05. The number stays reserved, so that an older reference does not point to another use case.

UC-23 — The companion feels

Priority: Must Frequency: Continuously Requirements: FR-27, FR-32, NFR-13 Phase: 1 in words; 2 in appearance Status: Approved

Description

The companion has feelings, and they come from what actually happens between it and the user. Following ALMA (3.2), it works at three time scales: a feeling caused by an event, a mood that moves with its feelings and lasts for hours or days, and a temperament, part of the character, that sets how far each event moves it, how fast the mood fades, and where it comes to rest. A feeling is a point in pleasure, arousal and dominance (PAD), shown to the user as a name — pleased, low, restless. The AI model only interprets what the user said as events; rules do the rest, so every feeling can be explained and replayed (BR-08, NFR-13).

Actors

  • Primary actor: Companion
  • Secondary actors: User
  • Systems involved: AI model, which interprets what the user said; Desk timer; Windows

Preconditions

  • A character with a temperament is in force (UC-01).
  • Moods are switched on (UC-18-A2).

Postconditions

  • On success: the emotion state has changed only through recorded events, each kept so that UC-17 can explain it and NFR-13 can replay it.
  • On failure: an event that cannot be recorded does not change the feeling. There is never a feeling without a recorded cause.

Main flow

  1. SystemRecords an event: something the user said, as interpreted by the AI model; something done together (UC-27); the user arriving, or being away; a message answered or passed over; the time of day.
  2. SystemAppraises the event under the temperament — how good or bad it is for the companion, and how much it concerns it — and turns it into a feeling, a point in PAD.
  3. SystemMoves its mood towards that feeling, by an amount the temperament sets.
  4. SystemLets the mood drift back towards the temperament’s resting mood as time passes without events.
  5. SystemCarries the mood in everything it says (phase 1) and in its expression and ambient motion (phase 2, BR-07), and lets it change what it does — how readily it starts conversations, and what about (UC-22).

UC-23-A1 — Being ignored

Branches from step 1. A message was passed over while the user was at the desk to see it, or days have gone by without the two of them talking.

  1. The companion grows sadder with each such event, down to a floor it does not go below (BR-08).
  2. When the user talks to it again, most of it is restored at once.
  3. It may say, once the user is back, that it missed them (UC-16); it never reproaches them for it (BR-04).
  4. A message that went unanswered because nobody was at the desk is not being ignored, and is not counted. Shutting the machine and going home is not a slight.
UC-23-A2 — The user asks how it feels

  1. The companion answers in character, naming its feeling and what brought it about, from the same recorded figures as UC-17-A2.
  2. It does not claim a feeling it does not have in order to please (BR-06).
UC-23-A3 — What the user said cannot be interpreted with confidence

Branches from step 1. The AI model cannot tell what the message means for the companion.

  1. No event is raised, and the feeling does not move on a guess.
  2. The flow ends.
UC-23-A4 — Being paused, moved or hidden

Branches from step 1. The user paused the companion (UC-15), or moved or hid it (UC-20).

  1. None of these is an event. The user arranging their own screen, or stopping the companion, is never something it feels anything about (BR-04, FR-27).
UC-23-E1 — The AI model is unavailable

Branches from step 1. What the user said cannot be interpreted.

  1. Events that need the model are not raised. Every other event — presence, messages answered or passed over, the time of day — is appraised as usual (NFR-08).

  • Never. A need, or anything the user must do for the companion (section 5). Wording meant to make the user feel guilty — “you never talk to me any more” (BR-04).
  • New in version 5.0. Version 4.0 and earlier forbade moods outright (FR-27 as it was). Being ignored is now something the companion feels (UC-23-A1).

UC-24 — Companion learns how the user works

Description

Where a pattern suggests it, it adjusts its own behaviour — when it speaks, how often, how long its replies are, how formal or playful its tone — by itself, telling the user what it did and offering to undo it. Only a change to the character itself is asked about first.

Main flow

  3. SystemWhere the pattern suggests it, adjusts its own behaviour — moves its remarks to the afternoon, keeps its morning replies short, lets its tone grow less formal.

Alternative flows

UC-24-A1 — A kind of message keeps being passed over

Branches from step 1. Three messages of the same kind in a row were passed over while the user was at the desk.

  1. The companion sends that kind less often and says so (FR-23).
  2. The user accepts that, switches the kind off altogether, or asks for it to be left as it was — in which case the count is reset and those three are not used as evidence again.
  3. Messages that went unanswered because nobody was at the desk are not counted.
UC-24-A2 — The change would be to its character

Branches from step 3. What the pattern suggests is a change to the character itself — its description or its temperament — rather than to its timing, length or tone.

  1. The companion describes the change it proposes to itself, and what prompted it, and asks.
  2. Only the user can apply it. On approval the previous character is kept, exactly as in UC-03, so the change can be undone.
  3. A refused proposal is never raised again.
UC-24-A3 — The user marks the pattern wrong

Branches from step 5, or from UC-12 at any time. The conclusion is wrong (UC-13-A1).

  1. The pattern is removed, and every adjustment built on it is undone.
  2. The companion does not draw the same conclusion again from the same evidence.
UC-24-A4 — The adjustment conflicts with something the user asked for

Branches from step 3. The user asked for this explicitly (UC-10).

  1. The user’s request wins. The companion does not adjust away something the user asked for.
UC-24-E1 — There is no evidence to show

Branches from step 1. The pattern cannot be supported by what was actually observed.

  1. Nothing is stored and nothing is adjusted. A conclusion it cannot justify is not drawn (BR-06).

  • Goal. G-07 measures how often what it learns is right.
  • New in version 5.0. Version 4.0 required approval for every change; the companion now asks only about its character.

UC-26 — Talk to the companion by voice

Priority: Should Frequency: Whenever the user prefers to speak Requirements: FR-38, FR-39, C-05, NFR-02 Phase: 2 Status: Draft

Description

The user can talk to the companion instead of typing, and hear it answer.   • On failure: nothing is sent, and the user can type instead.

  1. The system says that it did not catch that. Nothing is sent and nothing is guessed (BR-06).
  2. The use case ends.
UC-26-E3 — A speech engine is unavailable

Branches from step 5 or step 7. The engine that turns speech into text, or the one that gives the companion its voice, does not run.

  1. The system says which part is unavailable.
  2. The conversation goes on in text. Nothing else is affected (NFR-08).

  • Changed in version 6.2. The words are sent at once instead of waiting in the message box, and the key combination does not open the window.
  • Language. English only (C-06), for what is heard and for what is said.
  • New in version 6.0.

12. Glossary

TermDefinitionNotes
Actor A person in a particular role, another system, or a scheduled job that interacts with this system. Named by role, never by person.
Main flow The sequence of steps taken when everything goes as intended. Also called the “happy path”.
Companion Cognia acting on its own initiative — speaking, feeling or learning — as opposed to answering something the user said. An actor in its own right; see 6.1.
Character Who the companion is: its name, its role, its temperament, its appearance, its manner of speaking, and its history with the user. Not a tone setting, and since version 3.0 not only words. See FR-04 for what a character can never change.
Temperament The part of the character that sets how the companion feels: how far events move it, how quickly a feeling fades, and the resting mood it returns to. New in version 5.0. Designed by hand for each ready-made character (UC-01). BR-08.
Emotion state The companion’s current feeling, held as a point in pleasure, arousal and dominance (PAD) and shown as a name. New in version 5.0. Changed only by appraisal of recorded events. UC-23.
Mood The longer-lasting part of the emotion state, moved by each feeling and drifting back to the resting mood over time. New in version 5.0. Changes what the companion does as well as how it sounds. Can be switched off (UC-18-A2).
Appraisal Judging an event for what it means to the companion — good or bad, and how much it concerns it — and turning it into a feeling. New in version 5.0. Done by rules under the temperament; the AI model only interprets what the user said as events.
Continuity The companion remembering what the user told it and what it has learned of how they work, so that it is not starting from nothing each day. Serves the relationship, not record-keeping.
Learned pattern A conclusion the companion drew about how the user works, kept with the evidence for it and shown under “I noticed”. New in version 5.0. Used at once; can be marked wrong. UC-24.
Journal What the companion wrote, in its own voice, when it reflected on its recent memories. New in version 5.0; removed in version 6.1 with UC-25.
Commitment Removed in version 6.0. Something the user said they had to do, held until they said it was done. New in version 4.0; removed in version 6.0 with UC-21.
Opening the application The first time the main window is shown on a given day, a day beginning at 04:00 local time. Starting with Windows, and any later showing of the window that day, is not an opening. New in version 4.0. UC-16.
Presence Being on the screen while the user works, rather than sending them notifications. A pillar until version 5.0, now part of Agency; see 3.1.
Work session Removed in version 6.0. A stretch of work the user opened with one line and closed with one question. The core interaction until version 5.0, one shared activity in version 5, and removed in version 6.0 with UC-05 and UC-07.
Desk awareness The companion knowing whether the user is at the machine and which application is in the foreground, from idle time, lock state, presentation state and the application’s name — and nothing else. Off until switched on. Widened to four facts in version 5.0. UC-04.
Proactive message Anything the companion says or shows that the user did not ask for, including a speech bubble and any movement addressed to the user. Governed by BR-01 to BR-07; always explainable (UC-17). Ambient motion is not one.
Break suggestion A message suggesting a break after continuous time at the machine, at a moment the companion judges fitting. Called the break nudge before version 5.0. UC-22-A1.
Addressing motion Movement of the companion that is directed at the user — turning towards them, gesturing, showing a speech bubble. BR-07. Governed exactly as a spoken message is.
Ambient motion Movement addressed to nobody — breathing, blinking, a shift of posture, the expression of its mood, waiting or dozing while the user is away. New in version 5.0. Allowed at any time; never takes the focus or moves the companion. BR-07.
Quiet hours A period the user may set in which nothing is sent at all. BR-02. None by default since version 5.0. A locked machine and full-screen presenting are always quiet.
Base rule One of the eight rules in 8.1 that binds every use case and that no character can override. Called a “law” in version 1.0, where there were four.
Tool A fixed function the companion can use in a reply to get a real answer — a sum, a date, a figure from its own records — instead of guessing. New in version 6.0. UC-27, FR-40.
Tool server A program outside Cognia, added by the user, that offers the companion more tools through the Model Context Protocol. New in version 6.0. None by default; it may send what it receives off the machine. FR-41.
Desk timer The scheduled job that counts time at the machine, time since the user last talked to the companion, and the length of an absence. Called the session timer before version 6.0. See 6.1.
Local-only All data held on the user’s own disk, with no account, no server and no synchronisation. NFR-01 and C-02. A tool server the user adds is the one exception (FR-41).
