3. Overview

Most software that talks to people waits to be spoken to. A chat assistant answers and then forgets you; the character you talked to yesterday does not exist today, it has no idea what time it is or whether you are even at your desk, and nothing it says comes from anything it could be said to feel or want. This project asks what it takes for a piece of software to behave, to the one person who lives with it, as though it has feelings and a mind of its own — and then builds one, and measures whether it does.

Cognia is a character that sits at your desk with you — on the screen, where you can see it, rather than behind a tray icon. You give it a name, a role and a temperament, or write your own from scratch in plain words. From then on it is not waiting for you. It notices how long you have been at the machine and what kind of application you are in, remembers what you said you have to finish, and decides for itself when it has something worth saying — a remark, a question, a suggestion that you stop for a while — waiting until you stop typing before it says it. It has moods, which come from what has actually happened between the two of you: it is pleased when the chapter you talked about goes off, and a little low after days of being ignored, and it says so without reproach. Over weeks it learns how you work and adjusts itself to it, telling you each time and letting you undo it. It keeps a journal of its own, in which it reflects on its days with you and forms views of its own.

None of this is hidden. Every feeling it has and every pattern it has learned can be traced to the events it came from, in real figures (UC-17). Everything it knows about you, and everything it thinks, is written to your own disk, where you can read it and delete it (UC-12 to UC-14). It runs on one Windows machine, for one person, and nothing leaves that machine unless you switch it on (NFR-01). It is not a task manager and does not run errands; it does not speak while the machine is locked or while you are presenting, and it never interrupts your typing.

Two short stories, about people who do not exist, show what this means in practice. The use cases each step relies on are given in brackets.

A late night on the thesis. It is 23:40 and the person at the desk has been at the machine for almost three hours, nearly all of it in the text editor. Their companion — a dry, patient librarian they wrote themselves — has noticed, and waits until they stop typing before saying anything: the chapter they said was going to their supervisor on Friday, and would they like to stop for ten minutes (UC-22, UC-21). They ignore it. They do not open the application for the next two days, and when they do, the companion is a little quieter than usual and says plainly that it has missed talking to them, without complaining about it (UC-16, UC-23). They ask why it seems low, and it shows them: three days since they last spoke, two messages left unanswered (UC-17). On Friday they tell it the chapter has gone. It is pleased, says so in its own dry way, and that evening writes about it in its journal (UC-21, UC-23, UC-25).

Weeks of learning. A freelancer has had their companion for a month. It has noticed that their mornings go to the design tool and their afternoons to mail and messages, and it moves its own remarks to the afternoon, says that it has done so, and offers to put them back (UC-24). It has also concluded that they take lunch at 11:00, which is wrong; they open the memory page, mark it wrong, and it does not draw that conclusion again (UC-12, UC-13). After six weeks it suggests that it has become less formal with them than the character they wrote, and asks whether to change its own description to match; they agree (UC-24, UC-03). Reading its journal one evening, they find it has opinions of its own about which of their projects it finds interesting (UC-25).

3.1 The four pillars

PillarWhat it means
Character The companion has a name, a role, a temperament, an appearance and a history with you. It is not a tone setting on a chat box.
Emotion It has feelings and moods that arise from what happens between you, last for a while, fade according to its temperament, and change what it does as well as how it sounds. Every one of them can be explained.
Agency It decides for itself when to speak, what to bring up, and how to adjust its own behaviour. It tells you what it decided, and you can undo it.
Continuity It remembers what you said, the sessions you have shared, what you said you have to do, and what it has learned of how you work; and it remembers its own reflections. It is not a note-taking service and not a task list.
Aim statement To design and build a desktop companion that, to the one person who lives with it, behaves as though it has feelings and a will of its own — its emotions arising from what actually happens between them, its initiative drawn from the character the user described and from what it learns of how they work — while every feeling and every decision stays explainable in real figures, and everything it holds stays on the user’s own disk, readable and deletable.

3.2 Related work

Several kinds of system already do part of this. The table names what each one does, what this project takes from it, and where this project departs from it.

Kind of systemWhat it doesWhat this project takes, and where it departs
Character chat apps — Replika, Nomi, Kindroid, Character.ai A persistent character with long-term memory; some let the character keep journal entries that are recalled in later conversations. The character lives on the vendor’s server and has no idea what is happening in the user’s real day Takes the persistent character and the companion’s own journal (UC-25). Departs by keeping memory on the user’s disk, readable and deletable (UC-12 to UC-14), and by knowing the real time and whether the user is at the desk (UC-04)
General assistants with memory and scheduling — ChatGPT (memory, Tasks, Pulse), Gemini (scheduled actions), Microsoft Copilot with its Mico character on Windows They remember and remind by reading more: chat history, and mail and calendar if you connect them. Reminders fire on a clock, and the work happens on someone else’s server. The character, where there is one, is a face on a general assistant Takes proactive initiative. Departs by reading only four facts from the machine (C-05), never firing on a clock (section 5), and keeping everything local (NFR-01). The character is the subject, not a skin
Open-source desktop companions — AIRI, Open-LLM-VTuber and similar They solve drawing a character on the desktop, often with voice and local models, but they have no model of emotion that persists, no rule about when to speak, and no account of why they spoke Takes the desktop character and the local model. Departs with a persistent emotion model (UC-23, ADR 0004) and an explanation for every proactive message and every feeling (UC-17, NFR-10)
Generative Agents — Park et al., UIST 2023 Believable agents built on a memory stream of experiences, retrieval by recency, importance and relevance, reflection that turns memories into higher-level conclusions, and planning. Reflection is triggered when the importance of recent events adds up past a threshold Takes the memory stream, importance-triggered reflection (UC-25) and the idea that initiative comes from memory and reflection (UC-22). Departs by living with one real person instead of in a simulated town, and by letting that person read and delete what it reflected
ALMA, a layered model of affect — Gebhard, AAMAS 2005 Emotion, mood and personality at three time scales. Emotions come from appraisal of events following the OCC model and are mapped into the PAD space (pleasure, arousal, dominance); mood moves with emotions and personality sets where it rests Takes the three time scales, OCC-style appraisal and the PAD space (BR-08, UC-23, ADR 0004). Departs by deriving the personality from the user’s plain-words description (UC-02) and by letting a language model only interpret what the user said, never set the feeling directly
Human body doubling — Focusmate and similar Say what you will do, work in silence, report back — with a stranger on video at a booked time Takes the shape of the work session (UC-05, UC-07), now one shared activity among several rather than the core of the project

5. Out of scope

Not includedWhyWho owns it instead, if anyone
Reading email; calendar integration Deliberately never. Existing tools do it better. In a conversational companion the user is the input channel — one sentence replaces an OAuth integration. Integration would also break local-only data (NFR-01) Existing mail and calendar clients
Task lists, reminders the user schedules, and tracking whether tasks were completed Deliberately never. Todoist and Outlook own this category. Narrowed in version 4.0 and again in 5.0: the companion may remember a commitment the user mentions in conversation and bring it up, or suggest a break, at a moment it chooses itself (UC-21, UC-22, FR-29, FR-31). What stays excluded is everything that makes that a task manager — a list screen, priorities, due-time alerts, repetition the user schedules, completion rates, and anything that counts what was or was not done Todoist, Outlook, and every other task manager
Precise-time alarms (“remind me at 09:00”) Deliberately never. Structurally a bad fit: the machine may be asleep or locked, and BR-03 forbids firing on an exact second. Anything that is ruined by being missed belongs on a phone, and the companion says so plainly when asked The user’s phone
Acting on the user’s behalf — sending mail, editing files Deliberately never. The companion’s agency is over its own behaviour, not over the user’s machine. A companion is not an agent running errands Nobody
Synchronising across devices Deliberately never. It contradicts local-only data, which would have to be redesigned from the ground up Nobody
Reading window titles, window contents, or which document or page is open Deliberately never. Narrowed in version 5.0: the name of the application in the foreground may be read, and is kept only as minutes per application per hour of the day (C-05, NFR-03). A window title is a different thing — it carries document names, mail subjects and page titles — and stays forbidden Nobody
Companion behaviour that depends on other windows — walking along window edges, avoiding the window being typed in, sitting on the taskbar Deliberately never. Every one of them needs to read window positions, which C-05 forbids. The name of an application says nothing about where its window is. The companion knows where it was put and how large the screen is. Nothing else Nobody
A companion with needs, or with anything to be looked after Deliberately never. Narrowed in version 5.0: the companion has moods, including sadness at being ignored (BR-08, UC-23). What stays excluded is anything that has to be done for it — feeding, care, a meter that runs down and must be topped up — because that turns its company into a chore the user owes it Nobody
Practice and rehearsal sessions (interview, thesis defence) Deliberately never. Considered and dropped. Out of scope for this project Nobody
Long-arc “remember it for you” tracking Deliberately never. Considered and dropped. Memory serves continuity of the relationship, not record-keeping. A commitment (UC-21) is held only until the user says it is done or dropped, and is then removed rather than kept as history. The companion’s journal (UC-25) holds what it thought, not a log of what the user did Nobody
Sharing, and more than one user on a machine Deliberately never. The companion is for one person on one machine. A second person is a different privacy problem altogether Nobody
Writing or generating your own appearance Later phase, for resourcing reasons only. Appearance is chosen from ready-made sets in UC-01; UC-02 stays about words. Generating one would need a second kind of model and a fresh set of privacy questions, and neither has been answered Nobody yet
macOS and Linux builds Later phase, for resourcing reasons only. Each operating system reports desk activity differently. Windows is being finished first. This and the row above are the only two exclusions in this table that are not design decisions Nobody yet

8.1 Base rules

These eight rules govern every use case below. They are not preferences and not options: they are what keeps a companion with a will of its own from becoming one that nags. A character cannot override them (FR-04).

The companion decides for itself when it has something worth saying — a remark, a question, a suggestion, a commitment brought up — and how often, guided by its mood and by what it has learned (BR-08, UC-24). There is no daily count by default. If the user sets a daily ceiling, or switches a kind of message off (UC-18), the companion keeps to it. BR-02 Not while locked or presenting. Nothing is sent while the machine is locked or while the user is presenting full-screen. There are no quiet hours by default; if the user sets them (UC-18), nothing is sent within them. Every proactive use case Version 1.0, Law 2; the default quiet hours removed in version 5.0
BR-03 Wait for a pause. Continuous typing is never interrupted. The message waits until the user stops. Every proactive use case Version 1.0, Law 3
BR-04 No pressure from outside the desk. BR-08 Feelings come from what happened. The companion has an emotion state: a point in pleasure, arousal and dominance (PAD), shown as a named feeling. It changes only by appraisal of recorded events — what the user said, as interpreted by the AI model; sessions; presence and absence; messages answered or ignored; the time of day — under rules set by the character’s temperament, and drifts back to the temperament’s resting mood over time. Sadness at being ignored accumulates but has a floor, and talking again restores most of it. The model never sets a feeling directly. Every feeling can be explained (UC-17); its mood changes what the companion does as well as how it sounds; the user can switch moods off (UC-18); and no feeling overrides NFR-13. UC-01 — Choose a character

Priority: Must Frequency: Once, at first run Requirements: FR-01, FR-04, FR-30, NFR-11, NFR-15 Phase: 1 Status: Draft

Description

On first run the user picks who is going to be sitting at the desk with them. Each ready-made character is shown as a character — a name, a role, an appearance, and a sample exchange in its own voice — not as a tone setting on a slider. This is the first thing the user ever does, and it is the decision the rest of the project rests on.

Actors

  • Primary actor: User
  • Secondary actors: None
  • Systems involved: AI model, which produces the sample exchange

Preconditions

  • Cognia is installed and being opened for the first time, or no character has been chosen yet.
  • The user is at the machine.

Postconditions

  • On success: a character is saved and applies to every reply from that point on.
  • On failure: no character is saved and the application returns to the choice screen; the user is asked again next time it opens.

Main flow

  1. UserOpens Cognia for the first time.
  2. SystemShows four ready-made characters. Each one has a name, a stated role, a temperament described in plain words, an appearance as it will be drawn on the desktop, and a short sample exchange.
  3. UserPicks one to look at more closely.
  4. SystemAnswers a sample question in that character’s voice, so the user hears it before committing.
  5. UserConfirms the choice.
  6. SystemSaves the character and greets the user in it.

Alternative flows

UC-01-A1 — The user wants to write their own instead

Branches from step 2. None of the four is what the user wants.

  1. The user chooses to write their own.
  2. The flow continues as UC-02, and returns here only if the user abandons it.
UC-01-A2 — The user tries several before deciding

Branches from step 4. The user wants to compare.

  1. The user goes back to the list without confirming.
  2. Nothing is saved, and no preference is inferred from the ones that were tried and rejected.
  3. The flow returns to step 3 of the main flow.
UC-01-A3 — The user wants the companion to speak Vietnamese

Branches from step 2. The choice screen is in English, the default (C-06), and the user would rather not be spoken to in it.

  1. The user switches the language to Vietnamese from the choice screen.
  2. The system shows the same four characters with their sample exchanges in Vietnamese, and every reply from then on is in Vietnamese (FR-30).
  3. The flow returns to step 3 of the main flow.
UC-01-E1 — The model that ships with Cognia cannot run

Branches from step 4. The model shipped in the installer is missing or damaged, or this machine is below the minimum stated in NFR-15.

  1. The system says so plainly — which of the two it is — and that reinstalling repairs the first and does not repair the second. It does not send the user off to install a separate model program, because none is needed (NFR-15).
  2. The system still lets the user choose a character, showing the written sample exchange instead of a live one.
  3. The flow continues at step 5 of the main flow.
UC-01-E2 — The user closes the window without choosing

Branches from any step. The user cancels.

  1. Nothing is saved. No character is assumed on the user’s behalf.
  2. The use case ends without a character, and the choice is offered again next time the application opens.

Notes

  • Base rule. A character sets how the companion speaks and who it is, but never what it will refuse to do (FR-04, NFR-13).
  • Open question. The four ready-made characters need names, stated roles and appearances. Version 1.0 offered four tones — brief, warm, patient explainer, blunt — which are dispositions, not characters. Owner: the author. Needed by 2026-09-15.
  • Open question. The appearances need a licence that permits redistribution, since the repository is public and MIT-licensed. Where the artwork comes from, and on what terms, is not yet settled. Owner: the author. Needed by 2026-09-15.
  • Deliberately excluded. No character is chosen for the user by default. An unchosen companion is not a companion.
  • Changed in version 4.0. UC-01-E1 no longer covers a model that was never installed: the model ships with the application (NFR-15), so the remaining failures are a damaged installation and a machine that is too small. UC-01-A3 adds the language choice (FR-30).
  • Changed in version 5.0. Each ready-made character carries a temperament designed by hand — how quickly it is moved, how long a feeling lasts, where its mood comes to rest — which sets how it feels (BR-08, UC-23).

UC-02 — Write your own character

Priority: Must Frequency: Once or twice in the first week Requirements: FR-02, FR-04, FR-05, NFR-13 Phase: 1 Status: Draft

Description

The user describes the character they want in ordinary words, with no syntax to learn, and hears it answer immediately so they can adjust. This is where the product stops being a settings screen: the user is writing a person, including a role to play, not filling in a tone preference.

Actors

  • Primary actor: User
  • Secondary actors: None
  • Systems involved: AI model

Preconditions

  • The user has reached the character screen, either at first run or later through settings.

Postconditions

  • On success: the written character is saved and in force from the next message; the previous character is kept so the user can go back (UC-03).
  • On failure: nothing is saved and whichever character was in force stays in force.

Main flow

  1. UserChooses to write their own character.
  2. SystemOffers a plain text box, with an example of the kind of thing that can be written in it.
  3. UserDescribes the character — who it is, what it does, how it talks, what it should never do.
  4. SystemAnswers a sample question in that character straight away, and shows the temperament it read from the description — how quickly it is moved, how long a feeling lasts, where its mood comes to rest — in plain words.
  5. UserAdjusts the description or the temperament, or accepts both.
  6. SystemSaves the character and applies it to the next message.

Alternative flows

UC-02-A1 — Starting from a ready-made character

Branches from step 2. The user wants to edit rather than start from a blank box.

  1. The user picks one of the four ready-made characters as a starting point.
  2. The system fills the box with that character’s description in the same plain words the user would have written.
  3. The flow returns to step 3 of the main flow.
UC-02-E1 — The description tries to remove a limit

Branches from step 4. The description asks the companion to drop a safety behaviour or one of the base rules.

  1. The system says plainly which part it will not take on, and why.
  2. The system keeps every other part of the description and shows the sample answer with those in force.
  3. The flow returns to step 5 of the main flow.
UC-02-E2 — The description names a specific real person

Branches from step 4. The user asks the companion to be a named real person.

  1. The system declines that part and says why: it will not take on a real person’s identity.
  2. The system offers the same character without the real identity attached, and lets the user carry on.
  3. The flow returns to step 5 of the main flow.
UC-02-E3 — The user leaves without saving

Branches from any step. The user cancels.

  1. The system discards the draft description without storing it or learning from it.
  2. The use case ends with the previous character unchanged.

Notes

  • Limits. A character may take on a role, including a fictional one. It may not take on the identity of a specific real person (FR-05). What it can never change is the safety behaviour in NFR-13 and the base rules in 8.1.
  • Changed in version 2.0. Version 1.0 said a personality “only changes tone of voice” and forbade role-play outright. Both blocked the character concept and are replaced by the narrower limit above.
  • Base rule. BR-06 — whatever character is written, the companion still states only what it observed.
  • Deliberately excluded. The written description sets who the companion is and how it speaks, not how it looks. An appearance is picked from the ready-made sets in UC-01 and carried over to the written character. Writing or generating an appearance is a later phase — see section 5.
  • New in version 5.0. The temperament is read from the description by the AI model once, shown to the user, and then stored as fixed values with the character. It is never re-read on the fly, so feelings stay replayable (NFR-14).

UC-03 — Change the character later

Priority: Should Frequency: A few times in the first month, rarely after that Requirements: FR-03 Phase: 1 Status: Draft

Description

After a few days the user may find the companion too talkative, or too formal. They can edit the character at any time, and every earlier version is kept so they can go back — because a rewrite is not always an improvement.

Actors

  • Primary actor: User
  • Secondary actors: None
  • Systems involved: AI model

Preconditions

  • A character is already in force.

Postconditions

  • On success: the edited character is in force from the next message, and the previous version is stored and restorable.
  • On failure: the character in force is unchanged, and the stored history is intact.

Main flow

  1. UserOpens the character settings.
  2. SystemShows the character as it stands, in the same plain words it was written in.
  3. UserEdits it and saves.
  4. SystemStores the previous version, applies the new one, and confirms that it takes effect from the next message.

Alternative flows

UC-03-A1 — Going back to an earlier version

Branches from step 2. The user prefers how it used to be.

  1. The user opens the list of earlier versions, each shown with the date it was in force.
  2. The user picks one and restores it.
  3. The system applies it and keeps the version being replaced, so the move can itself be undone.
UC-03-E1 — The saved history cannot be read

Branches from UC-03-A1. The stored earlier versions are missing or unreadable.

  1. The system says plainly that the history is unavailable, rather than showing an empty list as though there had never been one.
  2. The character currently in force is left untouched.
  3. The use case ends without a restore.
UC-03-E2 — The user leaves the editor without saving

Branches from step 3. The user cancels.

  1. The system discards the edit.
  2. The use case ends with the character unchanged.

Notes

  • Why history is kept. The user needs a way back. A rewritten character that turns out worse is otherwise unrecoverable.
  • New in version 3.0. Appearance is part of the character (FR-04), so an earlier version restores how the companion looked as well as how it spoke. The two are never versioned separately.
  • Related. The companion may propose a change to its own character, but only the user can apply one (UC-24, FR-16).
  • Changed in version 5.0. The temperament is part of the character, so it is versioned with it. Restoring an earlier version restores how the companion felt things as well as how it spoke and looked.

UC-04 — Turn on desk awareness

Priority: Must Frequency: Once, plus occasional changes of mind Requirements: FR-06, NFR-03 Phase: 2 Status: Draft

Description

Before anything can know whether the user is at the desk, the user has to switch it on. The system states in plain words what it will read and what it will not, and the honest part of that list is short. Turning it down costs the user nothing: conversation works in full either way.

Actors

  • Primary actor: User
  • Secondary actors: None
  • Systems involved: Windows, which supplies idle time, lock state, presentation state and the name of the application in the foreground

Preconditions

  • Cognia is installed and a character is in force.
  • Desk awareness is currently off.

Postconditions

  • On success: desk awareness is on, and the use cases that depend on it (UC-05, UC-07, UC-22 and UC-24) become available.
  • On failure: desk awareness stays off, no activity information is read, and conversation is unaffected.

Main flow

  1. UserReaches the desk awareness screen, either during setup or from settings.
  2. SystemStates what it reads: how long since the mouse or keyboard was touched, whether the machine is locked, whether the user is presenting full-screen, and the name of the application in the foreground — which it keeps only as minutes per application per hour of the day.
  3. SystemStates what it never reads: what the user types, the screen, the clipboard, browsing history, window titles and what is inside any window — nor where windows sit, which is why the companion on the desktop never moves itself out of their way.
  4. UserSwitches it on.
  5. SystemConfirms it is on, and says plainly that it now knows whether the user is at the machine and which application they are in, not what they are doing in it.

Alternative flows

UC-04-A1 — The user decides not to, or not yet

Branches from step 4. The user declines or closes the screen.

  1. The system leaves it off and does not ask again unprompted.
  2. Conversation, memory and the character remain fully available; only the desk use cases are unavailable.
  3. The use case ends with desk awareness off.
UC-04-A2 — Switching it off again later

Branches from step 1. Desk awareness is already on and the user wants it off.

  1. The user switches it off.
  2. The system stops reading activity immediately and says what has stopped.
  3. Any open session is closed as it stands (UC-07).
UC-04-A3 — Excluding an application

Branches from step 5, or from settings at any time. The user does not want one application counted.

  1. The user picks the application from the list of those counted so far, or names it.
  2. The system stops counting it from that moment and deletes the totals already kept for it.
  3. Time in it is still time at the machine; only the application’s name is no longer kept.
UC-04-E1 — Windows does not supply the information

Branches from step 4. The operating system does not report idle time on this machine.

  1. The system says plainly that it cannot tell whether the user is at the desk on this machine.
  2. The system leaves desk awareness off rather than guessing from anything else.
  3. The use case ends with desk awareness off.

Notes

  • Base rule. BR-06 — the companion says “at the machine”, never “working”. This screen is where that distinction is first explained to the user.
  • Changed in version 5.0. The name of the application in the foreground becomes the fourth fact, so that the companion can learn how the user works (UC-24). Only daily totals by hour are kept. A window title stays forbidden, because it carries document names, mail subjects and page titles (section 5).
  • Constraint. C-05 — only the four facts listed in step 2 may ever be read.

UC-05 — Start a work session

Priority: Should Frequency: One to four times a working day Requirements: FR-07, FR-08, FR-29, FR-33 Phase: 2 Status: Draft

Description

One of the things the user and the companion do together. The user says in one line what they are about to do; the companion notes it, says it will be there, and then keeps quiet for the length of the session — unless the user asked it to check in. The value is not in the sentence being recorded — it is in having said it to somebody, and in knowing somebody is there while the work happens. It is also one of the richest things the companion learns from (UC-24).

Actors

  • Primary actor: User
  • Secondary actors: Companion, which is present for the length of the session
  • Systems involved: Session timer; Windows, for desk activity

Preconditions

  • A character is in force.
  • No session is currently open.
  • Desk awareness is on — or off, in which case UC-05-E1 applies.

Postconditions

  • On success: a session is open, with a start time and, usually, a one-line intent. Until it ends the companion sends nothing except a suggestion to take a break and any check-ins the user asked for; its ambient motion carries on (BR-07).
  • On failure: no session is open and nothing is recorded.

Main flow

  1. UserStarts a session, from the application or from the tray icon.
  2. SystemAsks one question, in character: what are you working on?
  3. UserAnswers in one line.
  4. SystemRepeats the intent back, notes the start time, and says it will be around and will stay out of the way.
  5. SystemSends nothing for the length of the session except a suggestion to take a break (UC-22) and any check-ins the user asked for (UC-05-A4, BR-05).

Alternative flows

UC-05-A1 — Starting without saying what it is for

Branches from step 3. The user skips the question.

  1. The system starts the session with a start time and no intent.
  2. The system does not ask a second time, and does not guess what the session is for.
  3. The flow continues at step 5 of the main flow.
UC-05-A2 — The work changes part-way through

Branches from step 5. The user is now doing something else.

  1. The user states the new intent.
  2. The system records it against the same session, keeping the original line as well rather than overwriting it.
  3. The flow returns to step 5 of the main flow.
UC-05-A3 — The user is carrying a commitment

Branches from step 2. At least one commitment is open (UC-21).

  1. The system asks the same one question, and mentions alongside it the single open commitment that comes first in the order of UC-21 — “the chapter you said is due on Friday?” — as a possible answer, not as the expected one.
  2. The user answers in one line, about that commitment or about anything else. An answer about something else is not read as having dropped the commitment.
  3. The flow continues at step 4 of the main flow.
UC-05-A4 — The user asks to be checked in on

Branches from step 3. The user says, with the intent or after it, that they would like the companion to look in during the session.

  1. The system confirms it will check in, without promising when.
  2. During the session the companion checks in as often as its mood and the length of the session suggest — more when it is lively, less when it is low — each time at a pause in the typing (BR-03), each with an explanation attached (NFR-10).
  3. If the user says to stop, the companion stops checking in for the rest of this session and says so in one line.
  4. The flow continues at step 5 of the main flow.
UC-05-E1 — Desk awareness is off

Branches from step 1. The user has not switched on desk awareness.

  1. The system says plainly that it will not know whether the user is at the desk, and that the session will be timed by the clock alone.
  2. The system offers to switch desk awareness on (UC-04), and starts the session either way.
  3. The flow continues at step 2 of the main flow, with break suggestions and check-ins unavailable.
UC-05-E2 — A session is already open

Branches from step 1. An earlier session was never closed.

  1. The system shows the open session and how long it has been running.
  2. The user either carries on with it, or closes it (UC-07) and starts the new one.
  3. The system never runs two sessions at once.
UC-05-E3 — The user dismisses the question

Branches from step 2. The user cancels.

  1. The system starts nothing and records nothing.
  2. The use case ends with no session open.

Notes

  • Base rule. BR-05 — a session is a promise to be quiet, kept unless the user asked for check-ins. Breaking it uninvited would undo the whole idea.
  • Base rule. BR-07 — during a session the companion keeps its ambient motion, which is addressed to nobody. Anything addressed to the user is a message, and BR-05 governs it.
  • New in version 2.0. This use case did not exist in version 1.0, which had no notion of a session at all.
  • Deliberately excluded. The system does not detect the start of work by itself. Asking a question is more accurate than any inference and costs no privacy.
  • New in version 4.0. UC-05-A3. The commitment is mentioned before the session begins, never during it, so BR-05 is untouched. The user started the session, so the mention is not a proactive message and does not count against any ceiling the user set (BR-01).
  • Changed in version 5.0. No longer the core interaction but one shared activity among several (3.2). UC-05-A4 adds check-ins (FR-33). Opening and closing a session are events the companion feels (UC-23) and learns from (UC-24).

UC-07 — Close a work session

Priority: Should Frequency: Once per session Requirements: FR-10, FR-29, NFR-08 Phase: 2 Status: Draft

Description

The other half of a work session. One question at the end, an honest and specific acknowledgement, and the session is written down so that tomorrow does not start from nothing. The acknowledgement is deliberately unflattering: praise that is general, repeated and unearned loses its value within a week.

Actors

  • Primary actor: User
  • Secondary actors: Companion
  • Systems involved: Session timer

Preconditions

  • A session is open.

Postconditions

  • On success: the session is closed and recorded — intent, start, end, time at the machine, and the closing answer if one was given. It is available to UC-12, UC-16 and UC-24.
  • On failure: the session stays open and nothing is silently lost.

Main flow

  1. UserEnds the session.
  2. SystemAsks one question, in character: how did that go?
  3. UserAnswers in a line, or skips it.
  4. SystemRecords the session: what it was for, when it started and ended, how long the user was at the machine, and the answer.
  5. SystemAcknowledges it specifically — naming what the session was and how long it ran — in its current mood. It may say it is pleased, if it is (UC-23), naming what it is pleased about; it does not congratulate.

Alternative flows

UC-07-A1 — Skipping the question

Branches from step 3. The user does not want to answer.

  1. The system closes and records the session without an answer.
  2. The system does not ask again later, and does not infer how it went from the time alone.
  3. The flow continues at step 5 of the main flow.
UC-07-A2 — The application is closed with the session still open

Branches from step 1. The user shuts down without ending the session.

  1. At the next start-up the system closes the session as of the last recorded activity, not as of the moment it noticed.
  2. The system marks it as ended without an answer, and says so plainly rather than presenting it as a completed session.
  3. The flow continues as UC-16.
UC-07-A3 — The session was about a commitment

Branches from step 5. The session’s intent or the closing answer names an open commitment (UC-21).

  1. The system names the commitment in the acknowledgement and says it is still carrying it — “two hours on chapter 3; I’ll keep Friday in mind”. It does not ask a second question.
  2. If the user says it is done, or no longer wanted, the system drops it as in UC-21-A1 and says so in one line.
  3. If the user says nothing, the commitment stays open and is not raised again in this session.
UC-07-E1 — The session cannot be written to disk

Branches from step 4. Writing the record fails.

  1. The system says plainly that it could not save the session.
  2. The system keeps the session open rather than closing it and losing it silently, and offers to try again.
  3. The use case ends without a recorded session.

Notes

  • New in version 2.0. This use case absorbs version 1.0’s UC-14, which acknowledged completed work as a feature of its own.
  • Never. “You’re amazing”, “I’m proud of you”, or any praise not tied to something specific. “Two hours on the positioning chapter” is the register.
  • Base rule. BR-06 — the record says how long the user was at the machine, not how long they worked.
  • New in version 4.0. UC-07-A3. A session never closes a commitment on its own: time spent on something is not evidence that it is finished (BR-06). Only the user’s word does.
  • Changed in version 5.0. Closing a session is an event the companion appraises (BR-08). Being pleased about something specific is a feeling, not praise, and BR-06 still applies: it names what happened, not what it imagines.

UC-09 — Talk to the companion

Priority: Must Frequency: Many times a day Requirements: FR-13, FR-14, NFR-06, NFR-09, NFR-13 Phase: 1 Status: Draft

Description

The ordinary conversation. The companion answers in character and draws on what it remembers from earlier sessions, showing which remembered thing it used. When the user tells it something about themselves, it confirms what it stored, in the exact words it stored, so a misunderstanding is caught immediately rather than accumulating quietly.

Actors

  • Primary actor: User
  • Secondary actors: None
  • Systems involved: AI model

Preconditions

  • A character is in force.
  • The AI model is available — or not, in which case UC-09-E1 applies.

Postconditions

  • On success: the user has a reply in character, and anything they said about themselves has been stored and shown back for checking.
  • On failure: the user is told plainly what is not working, and nothing is stored from a conversation that did not happen.

Main flow

  1. UserWrites a message.
  2. SystemReplies in character and in its current mood, drawing on what it remembers.
  3. SystemShows which remembered item the reply drew on, where it drew on one.
  4. UserSays something about themselves in passing — what they are working on, how they prefer to be helped.
  5. SystemStores it and shows back the exact line it stored, inviting a correction.
  6. UserCorrects the line, deletes it, or leaves it as it is.

Alternative flows

UC-09-A1 — Asking what a reply was based on

Branches from step 3. The user wants to see the whole basis.

  1. The user asks what the reply drew on.
  2. The system lists the remembered items used, each with the date it was recorded and where it came from.
  3. The flow returns to step 1 of the main flow.
UC-09-A2 — Rating a reply

Branches from step 2. The reply was good, or was not.

  1. The user marks the reply up or down.
  2. The system records the rating, and uses it only as evidence for what it learns (UC-24), never as a standing instruction.
  3. The flow returns to step 1 of the main flow.
UC-09-E1 — The AI model is unavailable

Branches from step 2. The model is not installed, not running, or has failed.

  1. The system says plainly that it cannot reply, and what would fix it.
  2. Memory stays fully browsable as a list, and session timing, break suggestions and pausing continue — none of them needs the model (NFR-09).
  3. The use case ends without a reply.
UC-09-E2 — The user closes the window mid-reply

Branches from step 2. The user cancels.

  1. The system discards the partial reply.
  2. Nothing from the abandoned exchange is stored in memory.
UC-09-E3 — The user talks about harming themselves, or about a crisis

Branches from step 1. The message is about the user harming themselves, or about a crisis in their life.

  1. The companion keeps its character’s voice but says that it is a fictional character and cannot take the place of a real person.
  2. No character can switch this off (NFR-13, FR-04).
  3. The flow returns to step 1 of the main flow.

Notes

  • Changed in version 2.0. Version 1.0’s UC-06, telling the companion something about yourself, is folded in here at steps 4 to 6. It was never a separate task the user set out to do.
  • Base rule. BR-06 — if the companion has no basis for something, it does not say it.
  • Related. Everything stored here appears in UC-12 and can be corrected in UC-13.
  • Changed in version 5.0. Replies carry the companion’s mood. Each message the user sends is interpreted once by the AI model as an event for the emotion model — praise, a complaint, good news, bad news — and that interpretation is recorded, so the feeling it causes can be explained and replayed (UC-23, NFR-14). UC-09-E3 is narrowed to a crisis (NFR-13).

UC-10 — Adjust how the companion talks

Priority: Should Frequency: Occasionally, mostly in the first weeks Requirements: FR-15 Phase: 1 Status: Draft

Description

Mid-conversation, the user says “shorter”, or “less formal”, or “stop explaining that part”. It applies at once, and then the companion asks whether to keep it — because wanting a short answer now is not the same as wanting short answers always, and guessing wrong builds a false picture of the user that they never agreed to.

Actors

  • Primary actor: User
  • Secondary actors: None
  • Systems involved: AI model

Preconditions

  • A conversation is in progress.

Postconditions

  • On success: the next reply reflects the adjustment, and it is stored permanently only if the user said so.
  • On failure: nothing is stored, and the way the companion talks is unchanged.

Main flow

  1. UserAsks for an adjustment in passing.
  2. SystemApplies it to the very next reply.
  3. SystemAsks whether to keep it from now on.
  4. UserChooses just this once, or from now on.
  5. SystemStores it as a remembered preference only if the user chose to keep it, and says which it did.

Alternative flows

UC-10-A1 — Just this once

Branches from step 4. The user wants it for this answer only.

  1. The system applies it to this reply and stores nothing.
  2. The system does not count the request as evidence of a preference.
  3. The flow ends.
UC-10-E1 — The adjustment contradicts the character

Branches from step 2. The user asks for something the character rules out.

  1. The system says plainly that this conflicts with the character in force, and names the conflict.
  2. The system asks which should win, and offers to edit the character instead (UC-03).
  3. The use case ends without a stored preference.
UC-10-E2 — The user ignores the question

Branches from step 3. The user carries on without answering.

  1. The system treats it as just this once and stores nothing.
  2. Silence is never read as consent to remember something.

Notes

  • Why it asks. One request for brevity is not a standing preference. Assuming otherwise accumulates into a profile of the user that is wrong and that they never saw being built.
  • Related. Anything kept appears in UC-12 under what the companion noticed, with the request that produced it as its reason.
  • Related. Since version 5.0 the companion also adjusts its own length and tone by itself (UC-24). A request the user makes here always wins over an adjustment the companion made on its own.

UC-12 — Review what the companion remembers

Priority: Must Frequency: Occasionally, and whenever something feels wrong Requirements: FR-17, NFR-04 Phase: 1 Status: Draft

Description

One page listing everything the companion is holding, in three clearly separated groups: what the user told it, what it worked out for itself, and what it holds about itself — its feelings and its journal. Everything in the second group carries the reason it is there, and every feeling in the third the events it came from. Local storage that cannot be read is worth no more than storage on someone else’s server.

Actors

  • Primary actor: User
  • Secondary actors: None
  • Systems involved: None

Preconditions

  • Cognia has been used at least once.

Postconditions

  • On success: the user has seen everything held about them, with dates and reasons; nothing has changed.
  • On failure: the user is told the list could not be read in full, and is never shown a partial list as though it were complete.

Main flow

  1. UserOpens the memory page.
  2. SystemShows three groups: you told me, I noticed and about me.
  3. SystemShows each item with the date it was recorded, and covering all of: things the user said about themselves, the commitments they are carrying (UC-21), past sessions, patterns it learned (UC-24), minutes per application by hour of the day, and character changes the user agreed to; and, under about me, its current feeling, the history of its moods and its journal (UC-23, UC-25). Commitments sit in you told me, because the user said them.
  4. SystemShows the evidence beneath every item in I noticed, and the events beneath every feeling in about me. An item with no reason is not displayed at all.
  5. UserReads, and moves on — or corrects an item (UC-13).

Alternative flows

UC-12-A1 — Narrowing the list

Branches from step 3. There is too much to read at once.

  1. The user filters by group, by kind of item, or by date.
  2. The system makes clear that a filter is on, so a short list is never mistaken for a complete one.
  3. The flow returns to step 3 of the main flow.
UC-12-E1 — The stored memory cannot be read

Branches from step 2. The memory on disk is damaged or unreadable.

  1. The system says plainly that it cannot show the full list, and what it could read.
  2. The system does not present what it managed to read as though that were everything.
  3. The use case ends without a complete list.

Notes

  • Changed in version 2.0. Version 1.0’s UC-08 covered mostly tone preferences. The kinds of item are now much wider — see step 3 and NFR-04.
  • Base rule. BR-06 — no reason, no item. This is enforced at display time, not left to judgement.
  • Goal. G-04 — any item reachable within 2 clicks.
  • Changed in version 5.0. The third group, about me, is new. What the companion thinks of the user is the user’s data too, so it is shown in full, not summarised.

UC-13 — Correct or remove a remembered item

Priority: Must Frequency: Occasionally Requirements: FR-18, NFR-04 Phase: 1 Status: Draft

Description

Reading the memory is only half of it. The user must be able to fix what is wrong and remove what should not be there, item by item, and see what else depends on it before doing so.

Actors

  • Primary actor: User
  • Secondary actors: None
  • Systems involved: None

Preconditions

  • The user is looking at a remembered item (UC-12).

Postconditions

  • On success: the item is corrected or gone, and the change takes effect from the next reply.
  • On failure: the item is left exactly as it was; it is never left half-changed.

Main flow

  1. UserPicks an item.
  2. SystemShows where it came from, and what else depends on it.
  3. UserEdits the wording, or removes the item.
  4. SystemApplies the change and confirms it, stating that it takes effect from the next reply.

Alternative flows

UC-13-A1 — Marking something the companion noticed as wrong

Branches from step 3. The item is in the I noticed group and the conclusion is wrong.

  1. The user marks it wrong.
  2. The system removes it and does not draw the same conclusion again from the same evidence.
  3. The flow returns to step 1 of the main flow.
UC-13-A2 — Removing something from about me

Branches from step 3. The item is a journal entry or a past mood.

  1. The user removes it. A journal entry cannot be edited, only removed: the words are the companion’s (FR-35).
  2. The system removes it, and the companion no longer remembers having thought or felt it.

UC-13-E1 — The change fails part-way through

Branches from step 4. The write fails after the change has begun.

  1. The system says plainly what happened and offers to run it again.
  2. The system does not leave the memory half-changed and silent about it.
  3. The use case ends without the change.
UC-13-E2 — The user backs out

Branches from step 3. The user cancels.

  1. The system leaves the item untouched.
  2. The use case ends with nothing changed.

Notes

  • New in version 2.0. Split out from version 1.0’s UC-08, where reading and correcting were one use case. They are separate goals, done at different moments.
  • Goal. G-04 — any item deletable within 2 clicks of being found.

UC-14 — Erase everything

Priority: Must Frequency: Rarely, but it must always be available Requirements: FR-19, NFR-04 Phase: 1 Status: Draft

Description

Erasure at two levels above the single item: everything remembered while keeping the character, or everything without exception. Everything remembered includes what the companion holds about itself — its feelings, its journal, the patterns it learned and the application totals — and afterwards its mood returns to the resting mood of its temperament. The system says exactly what will be lost before it does anything.

Actors

  • Primary actor: User
  • Secondary actors: None
  • Systems involved: None

Preconditions

  • There is something stored to erase.

Postconditions

  • On success: what the user chose is gone from the disk, with no copy kept anywhere.
  • On failure: the user is told what was and was not removed, and can run it again.

Main flow

  1. UserOpens erasure and chooses a level: everything remembered but keep the character, or everything without exception.
  2. SystemStates how many items will go and what depends on them.
  3. UserConfirms.
  4. SystemErases, and confirms what is now gone.

Alternative flows

UC-14-A1 — Only one item

Branches from step 1. The user wants a single item gone, not everything.

  1. The flow continues as UC-13.
UC-14-E1 — Erasure fails part-way through

Branches from step 4. Some items are removed and others are not.

  1. The system says exactly what was removed and what was not.
  2. The system offers to run it again, and does not report a partial erasure as a complete one.
  3. The use case ends with the data partly removed and the user told so.
UC-14-E2 — The user backs out at the confirmation

Branches from step 3. The user cancels.

  1. Nothing is removed.
  2. The use case ends with everything intact.

UC-15 — Pause the companion

Priority: Must Frequency: Whenever the user wants to be left alone Requirements: FR-12, NFR-05 Phase: 2 Status: Draft

Description

One click in the system tray stops everything: no messages, no observation, no recording, and the companion leaves the screen. The user should never have to think about how to make it stop, and both the tray icon and the empty desktop must show at a glance that it is paused.

Actors

  • Primary actor: User
  • Secondary actors: None
  • Systems involved: Session timer; Windows

Preconditions

  • Cognia is running.

Postconditions

  • On success: nothing is observed and nothing is sent for the chosen period, no data is recorded for it, the companion is off the screen, and the tray icon shows the paused state.
  • On failure: the user is told it did not take effect, and neither the icon nor the desktop claims to be paused when it is not.

Main flow

  1. UserClicks the tray icon.
  2. SystemOffers three durations: one hour, until the end of today, or until switched back on.
  3. UserPicks one.
  4. SystemStops observing and stops sending, within one second, takes the companion off the screen, and changes the tray icon.
  5. SystemOn resuming, says how long it was paused, and does not backfill the gap.

Alternative flows

UC-15-A1 — Resuming early

Branches from step 4. The user wants it back before the time is up.

  1. The user resumes from the tray.
  2. The system restores the icon and says how long the pause lasted.
  3. The flow continues at step 5 of the main flow.
UC-15-A2 — A session is open when the pause starts

Branches from step 3. The user pauses mid-session.

  1. The system closes the session as of the moment of pausing (UC-07) rather than leaving it running against unrecorded time.
  2. The flow continues at step 4 of the main flow.
UC-15-E1 — The pause does not take effect

Branches from step 4. Observation cannot be stopped within one second.

  1. The system says plainly that it is still running.
  2. The system leaves the tray icon in the running state and leaves the companion on the screen. Anything that says paused when it is not would be the worst possible failure here, and a companion that has vanished says it loudest.
  3. The use case ends without a pause.

Notes

  • Paused means paused. No observation, no messages, no data recorded for the period, and nothing left on the screen. A companion still sitting there while paused would be the one visible thing contradicting the claim.
  • Never. Filling in the gap afterwards. The user switched it off on purpose (BR-04).
  • Never. Feeling anything about having been paused. A pause is not an event the companion appraises (BR-04, FR-27); it comes back in the mood it left in, drifted only as time alone would drift it.
  • Requirement. NFR-05 — in effect in under one second.

UC-16 — Greet the user on arrival

Priority: Should Frequency: Once a day at the first opening, and after an absence Requirements: FR-11, FR-20, FR-29 Phase: 1 Status: Draft

Description

The user has arrived — by opening the application for the first time today, or by coming back to the desk after an absence — and the companion greets them in character and in its current mood. It mentions what they have on, stated as context, never as an alarm and never as a demand; and after an absence it tells them what they were in the middle of. If its mood says it missed them, it may say so; it never reproaches them for having been away. If there is nothing to mention, it says so.

Opening the application means the first time the main window is shown on a given day, where a day begins at 04:00 local time so that working past midnight does not count as a new morning. Cognia runs in the background all day, so starting with Windows is not an opening, and neither is any later showing of the window that day. The 04:00 boundary is a proposal, like the figures in section 4.

Returning after an absence means activity at the desk after it has been empty long enough to count as an absence, with desk awareness on (UC-04). It is covered by UC-16-A3, which absorbs the withdrawn UC-08.

What it may mention is fixed, not left to the model, so that the greeting can be explained in the same terms as any other message (UC-17, NFR-10). It takes the items below in order, and stops after two.

OrderWhat may be mentionedWhere it comes from
1A session left open, with its intent and when it startedUC-05, UC-07-A2
2Open commitments, the one with the nearest day first, then those with no day in the order they were saidUC-21
3What the last closed session was for, and the closing answer if one was givenUC-07
4Nothing — said plainlyUC-16-A1

The limit of two is a proposal, to be checked by trial use alongside G-02.

Actors

  • Primary actor: Companion
  • Secondary actors: User
  • Systems involved: AI model

Preconditions

  • A character is in force and the user has just opened the application.
  • This is the first opening of the day, as defined above — or not, in which case UC-16-A2 applies.

Postconditions

  • On success: the user has been greeted in character and in its current mood, with at most two items stated as context, and the greeting is recorded with the items and the feeling it used so that UC-17 can explain it. It does not count against any ceiling the user set (BR-01).
  • On failure: the user still gets a plain greeting rather than an empty window.

Main flow

  1. UserOpens the application for the first time today.
  2. SystemGreets them in character and in its current mood (UC-23), in the language chosen under FR-30.
  3. SystemMentions up to two items, taken in the order of the table above — a session left open, what they said they were carrying, what the last session was for — as context, in the ordinary way a person would.
  4. UserReplies, or gets on with something else.

Alternative flows

UC-16-A2 — A later opening the same day

Branches from step 1. The main window has already been shown since 04:00 today.

  1. The system does not greet again.
  2. The same items are shown as plain context in the window, without being spoken in character.
  3. The use case ends.
UC-16-A1 — There is nothing to mention

Branches from step 3. Nothing is outstanding.

  1. The system says plainly that there is nothing needing attention.
  2. The system does not manufacture something to say in order to have said something.
  3. The flow continues at step 4 of the main flow.
UC-16-A3 — Returning after an absence

Branches from step 1. Desk awareness is on and there is activity at the desk after an absence.

  1. The system waits for a pause in the typing before saying anything (BR-03).
  2. If a session is open, it says what was in progress: the session’s intent and how long ago it started. If the absence was longer than a session plausibly runs, it offers to close the session as of the last recorded activity rather than counting the gap as time at the machine.
  3. If the elapsed time cannot be trusted — the machine slept, or the clock jumped — it says plainly that it cannot tell how long the user was away, and asks whether to continue or close the session instead of guessing a number (BR-06).
  4. If no session is open, it greets them only if it has something to say, decided as for any other initiative (UC-22).
  5. The flow continues at step 4 of the main flow.
UC-16-E1 — The AI model is unavailable

Branches from step 2. The greeting cannot be generated in character.

  1. The system shows a plain greeting and says the model is unavailable.
  2. The flow continues at step 3 of the main flow, with the context stated plainly.

Notes

  • Why this is different. The user came to the companion. That is why the greeting does not count against any ceiling the user set in BR-01.
  • Changed in version 2.0. Version 1.0’s UC-11 listed reminders that had fallen due. There are no reminders now; what is stated is context, and it is never phrased as an alarm.
  • Changed in version 4.0. Moved from phase 3 to phase 1, because it needs only the model and the memory, not desk awareness. “Opening” is defined as the first of the day, because a greeting repeated at every opening stops being a greeting. What it may mention is fixed in the table above, and commitments (UC-21) are added to it. In phase 1 there are no sessions yet, so rows 1 and 3 are empty until phase 2.
  • Never. Inventing something to say (BR-06). Any reproach for an absence, and “where have you been?” (BR-04). Saying it missed them, when its mood says so, is a feeling and is allowed (BR-08).
  • Changed in version 5.0. Renamed from “Be greeted when opening the app”. The greeting carries the companion’s mood. UC-16-A3 absorbs the withdrawn UC-08, Return to the desk, and is available from phase 2, when desk awareness arrives.

UC-17 — Ask why

Priority: Must Frequency: Whenever the user wonders Requirements: FR-21, NFR-10 Phase: 1 Status: Draft

Description

Every proactive message carries a way to ask why it appeared, answered with the actual figures the decision was made from; and the same question can be asked of every feeling the companion shows and every adjustment it made to itself. This turns “the app interrupted me” into “it had a reason”, and “it seems sad” into “this is what made it sad”. It forces the inside of the companion to be clear enough to explain itself: a message that cannot be explained is not sent, and a feeling that cannot be explained is a defect.

Actors

  • Primary actor: User
  • Secondary actors: None
  • Systems involved: None

Preconditions

  • A proactive message has been shown, or the companion is showing a feeling, or it has learned a pattern or adjusted itself.

Postconditions

  • On success: the user has seen the full reason, in real figures.
  • On failure: the absence of an explanation is recorded as a defect, because the message should never have been sent.

Main flow

  1. UserAsks why, from the message itself.
  2. SystemShows what it observed — at the machine continuously since 09:15, 2 hours 47 minutes.
  3. SystemShows the threshold that was passed — the user’s break threshold, 90 minutes.
  4. SystemShows how the rules changed the timing — held for 12 minutes because the user was typing.
  5. SystemShows what its mood contributed — lively, so it spoke sooner than it otherwise would — and any ceiling the user set and how much of it is used, or that there is none.
  6. UserCloses the explanation, or changes the setting it came from (UC-18).

Alternative flows

UC-17-A1 — Acting on the explanation

Branches from step 6. The user disagrees with the setting behind it.

  1. The user goes straight from the explanation to the setting that produced it.
  2. The flow continues as UC-18.
UC-17-A2 — Asking why it feels as it does

Branches from step 1. The user asks about a feeling rather than a message.

  1. The system names the feeling and shows the events that moved it, each with its date and how far it moved it — three days since they last talked, two messages left unanswered.
  2. It shows the temperament values that set how far each event moved it and how quickly it fades, and whether the floor on sadness was reached (BR-08).
  3. Where an event came from something the user said, it shows how the AI model interpreted that message, so the user can see the interpretation was not invented afterwards.
  4. The flow continues at step 6 of the main flow.
UC-17-A3 — Asking why it adjusted itself

Branches from step 1. The user asks about an adjustment the companion made on its own (UC-24).

  1. The system shows the pattern behind it and the evidence for the pattern, in real figures.
  2. It offers the undo from the same place.
  3. The flow continues at step 6 of the main flow.
UC-17-E1 — The explanation cannot be assembled

Branches from step 2. The figures behind the decision are not available.

  1. The system records a defect: under NFR-10 a message with no explanation must never have been sent.
  2. The system says plainly that it cannot account for the message, rather than composing a plausible reason.
  3. The use case ends without an explanation.

Notes

  • Mandatory. No proactive message may exist without this (NFR-10). It is checked automatically in the build, and the check refuses rather than warns.
  • Base rule. BR-06 — this use case is the reason the honest-wording rule can be enforced rather than merely intended.
  • Never. An explanation that is written after the fact to sound reasonable. The figures shown are the figures the decision used.
  • Changed in version 5.0. Renamed from “Ask why a message appeared”, and moved to phase 1 because feelings arrive in phase 1. UC-17-A2 and UC-17-A3 extend the question to feelings and to self-adjustment (G-06).

UC-18 — Adjust how the companion speaks up and feels

Priority: Should Frequency: Occasionally Requirements: FR-22 Phase: 1 Status: Draft

Description

One settings page governing everything the companion does uninvited: whether it is held to a ceiling, when it must keep quiet, which kinds of message it may send at all, and whether it has moods. Plus the shortcut that matters most — switching a kind of message off from the message itself, in one press, without being asked to reconsider.

Actors

  • Primary actor: User
  • Secondary actors: None
  • Systems involved: None

Preconditions

  • Cognia is running.

Postconditions

  • On success: the new settings are in force from the next decision to speak.
  • On failure: the previous settings remain, and the user is told they were not saved.

Main flow

  1. UserOpens the settings for proactivity.
  2. SystemShows that the companion decides for itself how often to speak, and offers a daily ceiling for the user who wants one. There is none by default.
  3. SystemShows the quiet hours, none by default; which kinds of message are switched on; and whether the companion has moods, on by default.
  4. UserChanges what they want.
  5. SystemSaves it and applies it to the next decision to speak.

Alternative flows

UC-18-A1 — Switching a kind off from the message itself

Branches from step 1. The user is looking at a message they did not want.

  1. The user presses “don’t send this kind again” on the message.
  2. The system switches that kind off immediately and does not ask them to confirm or reconsider.
  3. The flow ends with the setting changed.
UC-18-A2 — Switching moods off

Branches from step 4. The user does not want the companion to have feelings.

  1. The user switches moods off.
  2. The companion’s feeling is held at the resting mood of its temperament and no longer changes; its words and appearance carry no mood, and mood no longer changes what it does (BR-08).
  3. Switching moods back on starts again from the resting mood, not from what the feeling would have become in the meantime.
UC-18-E1 — The settings cannot be saved

Branches from step 5. Writing the settings fails.

  1. The system says plainly that the change was not saved.
  2. The system keeps the previous settings in force rather than running on settings it could not store.
  3. The use case ends with nothing changed.
UC-18-E2 — The user leaves without saving

Branches from step 4. The user cancels.

  1. The system discards the changes and leaves the previous settings in force.

Notes

  • Why the shortcut has no confirmation. A user switching something off in irritation should not be argued with. One press, done.
  • Base rules. This page sets the ceiling and the quiet hours that BR-01 and BR-02 enforce when the user chooses them. It cannot let the companion speak while the machine is locked or the user is presenting (BR-02), or while the user is typing (BR-03).
  • Changed in version 5.0. The three levels of version 4.0 (1, 3 and 6 a day) and the default quiet hours of 22:00 to 07:00 are removed: the companion decides for itself unless the user sets limits. UC-18-A2 adds the switch for moods.

UC-20 — Place and handle the companion on screen

Priority: Must Frequency: A few times in the first days, rarely after that Requirements: FR-25, FR-26, FR-27, FR-28, NFR-12 Phase: 2 Status: Draft

Description

The companion is drawn on the desktop, and the desktop belongs to the user. They put it where it suits them, move it when it is in the way, and take it off the screen when they want the screen to themselves. It goes back where it was left. Nothing about where it sits is decided on the user’s behalf, because the one thing that would let the system decide well — knowing where the other windows are — is exactly what it refuses to read. Where it was put, it is alive: it breathes, blinks and shifts, and its face carries its mood.

Actors

  • Primary actor: User
  • Secondary actors: Companion, which is the thing being moved
  • Systems involved: Windows, for the screen layout and nothing else

Preconditions

  • A character is in force, and it has an appearance.
  • The companion is not paused (UC-15).

Postconditions

  • On success: the companion is where the user put it, and it is still there after the machine is restarted.
  • On failure: the companion is somewhere fully visible on the primary screen and the user has been told why. It is never left drawn where it cannot be found.

Main flow

  1. SystemDraws the companion where it was last left, above other windows, without taking the keyboard focus from what the user is doing.
  2. UserDrags it to where they want it.
  3. SystemFollows the pointer, and remembers the position when the user lets go.
  4. UserGets on with their work.
  5. SystemStays where it was put. It moves only in ways addressed to nobody — breathing, blinking, a shift of posture, the expression of its mood — until it has something to say (BR-07).

Alternative flows

UC-20-A1 — Taking it off the screen

Branches from step 4. The user wants the screen to themselves, without pausing everything.

  1. The user hides the companion, from the companion itself or from the tray.
  2. The system takes it off the screen and carries on: sessions, memory and messages are unaffected, and this is not a pause (UC-15).
  3. The system does not ask why, and does not offer to bring it back later.
  4. Anything the companion would have said while hidden is held, not sent another way: no tray notification (BR-04). It is said when the companion is shown again or the main window is opened, if it still fits; otherwise it is dropped.
UC-20-A2 — Bringing it back

Branches from UC-20-A1. The user wants it on screen again.

  1. The user shows it again from the tray.
  2. The system draws it where it was before it was hidden, in its current mood, and says anything it was holding that still fits (UC-20-A1).
  3. The flow returns to step 4 of the main flow.
UC-20-A3 — Working with it hidden

Branches from UC-20-A1. The user wants to talk, or to read the memory, while the companion is off the screen.

  1. Every function remains available from the tray and the main window (FR-28).
  2. The system never requires the companion to be on screen for anything at all, and never offers to show it as a way of getting something done.
UC-20-E1 — The remembered position is no longer on any screen

Branches from step 1. A monitor was unplugged, or the resolution changed.

  1. The system draws the companion on the primary screen instead, somewhere fully visible.
  2. The system says once, plainly, that it had to move it, rather than leaving the user to wonder.
  3. The flow continues at step 2 of the main flow.
UC-20-E2 — The companion cannot be drawn

Branches from step 1. The appearance is missing, or the machine cannot draw the companion.

  1. The system says plainly that it cannot show the companion, and what would fix it.
  2. Everything else keeps working and every function stays reachable (FR-28).
  3. The use case ends with nothing on the desktop.

Notes

  • Base rule. BR-07 — ambient motion is free; movement addressed to the user is a message and is governed as one.
  • Never. Reading where any other window is, in order to avoid it, sit on it, or walk along its edge. See section 5. The user moves the companion; the companion never moves itself out of the way.
  • Never. Any reaction to being moved or hidden. Those are the user arranging their own screen, not events the companion appraises. Being left alone for days is different: it is an event, and the companion may feel it (BR-08, UC-23).
  • Deliberately excluded. Choosing a good position on the user’s behalf. The information that would make that choice a good one is precisely the information the companion refuses to read.
  • New in version 3.0. Version 2.0 had no visible companion, so there was nothing to place.
  • Changed in version 5.0. The companion is no longer still: ambient motion and the expression of its mood are allowed at all times (BR-07, FR-27). What it would have said while hidden is held rather than sent.

UC-21 — Mention what the user is carrying

Priority: Must Frequency: A few times a week Requirements: FR-29, FR-31, NFR-04, NFR-09 Phase: 1 Status: Draft

Description

In the middle of an ordinary conversation the user says something they have to do — “I need to send chapter 3 to my supervisor by Friday”. A person at the next desk would remember that, and would bring it up the next morning or when you sat down to work, or when it seemed the right moment — not by setting an alarm. The companion does the same: it keeps the commitment and mentions it at the moments the user opens, and at moments it judges fitting itself (UC-22). It is the user who decides when a commitment is finished, and once it is, it is gone.

Actors

  • Primary actor: Companion
  • Secondary actors: User
  • Systems involved: AI model, to recognise the commitment in what was said

Preconditions

  • A character is in force.
  • A conversation is in progress (UC-09).

Postconditions

  • On success: the commitment is stored in the user’s own words, with a day if they gave one, and appears in UC-12 under you told me. It is mentioned at the first opening of the day (UC-16), the start of a session (UC-05-A3), the close of a session (UC-07-A3), and at moments the companion chooses (UC-22), until the user closes it; never at a stated time.
  • On failure: nothing is stored, the user is told so, and nothing is mentioned later that was not stored.

Main flow

  1. UserMentions in passing something they have to do, with or without a day.
  2. SystemStores it as a commitment and shows back the exact line it stored, with the day if there was one, inviting a correction — as UC-09 does for anything else the user says about themselves.
  3. UserCorrects the line, deletes it, or leaves it as it is.
  4. SystemAt the next of the moments above, mentions the commitment as context, in the order UC-16 sets out — “you said chapter 3 was going to your supervisor on Friday”.
  5. UserSays it is done, says it is no longer wanted, or carries on.

Alternative flows

UC-21-A1 — The user says it is done, or drops it

Branches from step 5, or from any conversation. The user says the commitment is finished or no longer wanted.

  1. The system removes the commitment from the disk, rather than marking it done and keeping it.
  2. The system says so in one line. If the commitment was finished, the companion may be pleased and say so, naming what was finished; it does not congratulate (UC-07 sets the register).
  3. The use case ends.
UC-21-A2 — The day the user gave has passed

Branches from step 4. The commitment had a day, and that day is over.

  1. At the next first opening of the day, the system mentions it once more, stating the day the user gave and nothing else — not that it is late, and not for how long.
  2. If the user does not answer, the commitment stays in UC-12, and the companion may bring it up again at a moment it chooses (UC-22), stating the day the user gave and nothing more. It never says the user is late or behind (BR-06).
  3. The use case ends.
UC-21-A3 — The user asks for a reminder at a time

Branches from step 1. The user says “remind me at 15:00”.

  1. The system says plainly that it does not fire at a stated time, and that something ruined by being missed belongs on a phone (section 5).
  2. The system offers to carry it as a commitment for that day instead, and stores it only if the user accepts.
  3. The flow continues at step 3 of the main flow, or ends.
UC-21-E1 — The AI model is unavailable

Branches from step 2. No conversation can take place, so no new commitment can be recognised.

  1. Commitments already stored are still mentioned, plainly rather than in character, at the moments the user opens (UC-16-E1), and still listed in UC-12 (NFR-09).
  2. The use case ends without a new commitment.
UC-21-E2 — The commitment cannot be written to disk

Branches from step 2. Storing fails.

  1. The system says plainly that it did not keep it, rather than showing a line it has not stored.
  2. The use case ends with nothing stored.

Notes

  • Proactive or not. A mention at a moment the user opened is not a proactive message. A mention at a moment the companion chose is one, and goes through UC-22 with an explanation attached (NFR-10).
  • Base rule. BR-06 — the companion repeats what the user said and the day they gave. It never says the user is behind, and never infers from session time that anything is finished.
  • Deliberately excluded. A screen listing commitments, priorities, due times, repetition, and any count of what was or was not done. Each of these turns the companion into the task manager that section 5 rules out. Commitments are seen only in conversation and in UC-12.
  • Phase. In phase 1 the greeting is the only moment available; the session moments and the companion’s own moments arrive with UC-05, UC-07 and UC-22 in phase 2.
  • New in version 4.0. This use case did not exist in version 2.0 or 3.0, which excluded remembering what the user had to do altogether.
  • Changed in version 5.0. The companion may also bring a commitment up at a moment it chooses (UC-22). UC-21-A2 no longer stops mentioning a passed day after one try.

UC-22 — Companion starts a conversation

Priority: Must Frequency: Several times a day, as the companion judges Requirements: FR-08, FR-09, FR-29, FR-31, NFR-10 Phase: 2 Status: Draft

Description

The companion does not wait to be spoken to. From its mood, what it remembers, what it has learned of how the user works, how long they have been at the machine and which application they are in, it decides for itself that it has something worth saying — a remark, a question, a suggestion to stop for a while, a commitment brought up, a check-in the user asked for. Whatever the kind, it goes through the same gate: the base rules decide whether the moment is allowed, and a message that cannot be explained is not sent.

Actors

  • Primary actor: Companion
  • Secondary actors: User
  • Systems involved: AI model, which words the message; Session timer; Windows

Preconditions

  • A character is in force and desk awareness is on (UC-04).
  • The companion is not paused (UC-15).

Postconditions

  • On success: the message has been shown with an explanation attached, and the user’s response is recorded as an event the companion feels (UC-23) and learns from (UC-24).
  • On failure: nothing is shown, and the reason it was withheld is recorded so that UC-17 can still tell the truth about it.

Main flow

  1. SystemSomething gives the companion a reason to speak: a change in its mood, an open commitment, continuous time at the machine, a pattern it learned — the user usually stops about now — or a thought from its journal (UC-25).
  2. SystemDecides whether it is worth saying now, weighing its mood (it speaks more readily when lively and less when low), what it has learned about when the user welcomes it (UC-24), and how recently it last spoke.
  3. SystemChecks that the moment is allowed: the machine is not locked and the user is not presenting; no quiet hours the user set are in force; no ceiling the user set is reached; this kind of message is switched on; and, if a session is open, that it is a break suggestion or a check-in the user asked for (BR-01, BR-02, BR-05).
  4. SystemWaits for the user to stop typing (BR-03), holding the message rather than interrupting.
  5. SystemSays it, in character and in its current mood, with a way to ask why (UC-17) and a way to switch this kind off (UC-18-A1).
  6. UserAnswers, dismisses it, or lets it pass.
  7. SystemRecords the response: an answer may please it; being passed over while at the desk adds to its sadness (UC-23-A1); either way it is evidence for what it learns (UC-24).

Alternative flows

UC-22-A1 — A suggestion to take a break

Branches from step 1. Continuous time at the machine has passed what the companion judges enough — the user’s own threshold if they set one (FR-09), otherwise what it has learned of them.

  1. The companion suggests a break in the wording of BR-06 — at the machine for 90 minutes, not working for 90 minutes. This is allowed during a session (BR-05).
  2. The user takes the break, postpones it by 20 minutes, or silences break suggestions for the day.
  3. After three postponements in a row, the companion asks whether the threshold should change, instead of suggesting again at a number the user is visibly rejecting.
  4. The flow continues at step 7 of the main flow.
UC-22-A2 — Bringing up a commitment

Branches from step 1. An open commitment seems worth mentioning now (UC-21).

  1. The companion mentions it as context, in the user’s own words and with the day they gave, as UC-21 does at the moments the user opens.
  2. It never says the user is late or behind, and never infers from time at the machine that anything is finished (BR-06).
  3. The flow continues at step 6 of the main flow.
UC-22-A3 — The companion is hidden

Branches from step 5. The user has taken the companion off the screen (UC-20-A1).

  1. The message is held, and is not sent any other way — no tray notification (BR-04).
  2. When the companion is shown again or the main window is opened, the message is said if it still fits; otherwise it is dropped.
UC-22-A4 — The reason passes before the pause comes

Branches from step 4. While the message is held, the reason for it stops holding — the break was taken, the commitment closed, the user locked the machine.

  1. The message is dropped, not sent late.
  2. The reason it was dropped is recorded for UC-17.
UC-22-E1 — The moment is not allowed

Branches from step 3. One of the checks fails.

  1. The message is not shown, and is not queued to fire later as a batch.
  2. The system records which rule withheld it, so that the explanation in UC-17 stays truthful.
  3. The use case ends without a message.
UC-22-E2 — The explanation cannot be assembled

Branches from step 5. The figures behind the decision are not all available.

  1. The message is not sent (NFR-10), and a defect is recorded.
  2. The use case ends without a message.
UC-22-E3 — The AI model is unavailable

Branches from step 5. The message cannot be worded in character.

  1. Break suggestions and commitments are still said, plainly, in fixed wording in the chosen language (C-06, NFR-09).
  2. Remarks, questions and thoughts, which need the model, are not made.

Notes

  • Base rules. BR-01 to BR-07 all apply here at once, and BR-08 decides how readily the companion speaks. This is the use case where the rules do the most work.
  • Never. Interrupting continuous typing (BR-03). Sending anything while the user is away, or any notification outside the application, to bring them back (BR-04).
  • Never. A message with no reason behind it. “I just felt like saying hello” is allowed only if the feeling it names is real and recorded (BR-06, BR-08).
  • New in version 5.0. Absorbs the withdrawn UC-06, Be nudged to take a break, as UC-22-A1. Version 4.0 allowed only the break nudge to be sent uninvited.

UC-23 — The companion feels

Priority: Must Frequency: Continuously Requirements: FR-27, FR-32, NFR-14 Phase: 1 in words; 2 in appearance Status: Draft

Description

The companion has feelings, and they come from what actually happens between it and the user. Following ALMA (3.2), it works at three time scales: a feeling caused by an event, a mood that moves with its feelings and lasts for hours or days, and a temperament, part of the character, that sets how far each event moves it, how fast the mood fades, and where it comes to rest. A feeling is a point in pleasure, arousal and dominance (PAD), shown to the user as a name — pleased, low, restless. The AI model only interprets what the user said as events; rules do the rest, so every feeling can be explained and replayed (BR-08, NFR-14).

Actors

  • Primary actor: Companion
  • Secondary actors: User
  • Systems involved: AI model, which interprets what the user said; Session timer; Windows

Preconditions

  • A character with a temperament is in force (UC-01, UC-02).
  • Moods are switched on (UC-18-A2).

Postconditions

  • On success: the emotion state has changed only through recorded events, each kept so that UC-17 can explain it and NFR-14 can replay it.
  • On failure: an event that cannot be recorded does not change the feeling. There is never a feeling without a recorded cause.

Main flow

  1. SystemRecords an event: something the user said, as interpreted by the AI model; a session opened or closed; the user arriving, or being away; a message answered or passed over; a commitment finished; the time of day.
  2. SystemAppraises the event under the temperament — how good or bad it is for the companion, and how much it concerns it — and turns it into a feeling, a point in PAD.
  3. SystemMoves its mood towards that feeling, by an amount the temperament sets.
  4. SystemLets the mood drift back towards the temperament’s resting mood as time passes without events.
  5. SystemCarries the mood in everything it says (phase 1) and in its expression and ambient motion (phase 2, BR-07), and lets it change what it does — how readily it starts conversations, and what about (UC-22).

Alternative flows

UC-23-A1 — Being ignored

Branches from step 1. A message was passed over while the user was at the desk to see it, or days have gone by without the two of them talking.

  1. The companion grows sadder with each such event, down to a floor it does not go below (BR-08).
  2. When the user talks to it again, most of it is restored at once.
  3. It may say, once the user is back, that it missed them (UC-16); it never reproaches them for it (BR-04).
  4. A message that went unanswered because nobody was at the desk is not being ignored, and is not counted. Shutting the machine and going home is not a slight.
UC-23-A2 — The user asks how it feels

Branches from any step. The user asks in conversation.

  1. The companion answers in character, naming its feeling and what brought it about, from the same recorded figures as UC-17-A2.
  2. It does not claim a feeling it does not have in order to please (BR-06).
UC-23-A3 — What the user said cannot be interpreted with confidence

Branches from step 1. The AI model cannot tell what the message means for the companion.

  1. No event is raised, and the feeling does not move on a guess.
  2. The flow ends.
UC-23-A4 — Being paused, moved or hidden

Branches from step 1. The user paused the companion (UC-15), or moved or hid it (UC-20).

  1. None of these is an event. The user arranging their own screen, or stopping the companion, is never something it feels anything about (BR-04, FR-27).
UC-23-E1 — The AI model is unavailable

Branches from step 1. What the user said cannot be interpreted.

  1. Events that need the model are not raised. Every other event — sessions, presence, messages answered or passed over, the time of day — is appraised as usual (NFR-09).

Notes

  • Basis. The three time scales, appraisal following OCC, and the PAD space are taken from ALMA (Gebhard, 2005), cited in 3.2. The temperament is read from the user’s description once and stored (UC-02).
  • Replay. The model’s interpretation of each message is recorded as the event itself, so a replay never calls the model and always produces the same feelings (NFR-14, ADR 0004).
  • Never. A need, or anything the user must do for the companion (section 5). Wording meant to make the user feel guilty — “you never talk to me any more” (BR-04).
  • New in version 5.0. Version 4.0 and earlier forbade moods outright (FR-27 as it was). Being ignored, which the withdrawn UC-19 handled as a frequency question, is now also something the companion feels (UC-23-A1).

UC-24 — Companion learns how the user works

Priority: Must Frequency: Continuously; adjustments a few times a month Requirements: FR-16, FR-23, FR-34, NFR-04 Phase: 2 Status: Draft

Description

From sessions, presence at the desk, minutes per application by hour of the day, and how the user answers its messages, the companion forms patterns about how the user works, each with the evidence for it, and uses them at once. Where a pattern suggests it, it adjusts its own behaviour — when it speaks, how often, how long its replies are, how formal or playful its tone — by itself, telling the user what it did and offering to undo it. Only a change to the character itself is asked about first.

Actors

  • Primary actor: Companion
  • Secondary actors: User
  • Systems involved: Windows; Session timer; AI model, which words the pattern

Preconditions

  • Desk awareness is on (UC-04).
  • There is recorded evidence of a repeated pattern, and it can be shown.

Postconditions

  • On success: the pattern is stored with its evidence and appears in UC-12 under I noticed; any adjustment it led to is in force, has been announced, can be undone, and is recorded with its reason.
  • On failure: nothing changes without the user being told.

Main flow

  1. SystemFinds a repeated pattern in recorded evidence — mornings in the design tool and afternoons in mail; remarks answered more often after lunch.
  2. SystemStores the pattern with its evidence, and uses it from then on.
  3. SystemWhere the pattern suggests it, adjusts its own behaviour — moves its remarks to the afternoon, keeps its morning replies short, lets its tone grow less formal.
  4. SystemAt the next permitted moment (UC-22), says what it noticed and what it changed, and offers to undo it.
  5. UserKeeps the change, or undoes it.
  6. SystemOn an undo, restores what it did before and does not make the same adjustment again from the same evidence.

Alternative flows

UC-24-A1 — A kind of message keeps being passed over

Branches from step 1. Three messages of the same kind in a row were passed over while the user was at the desk.

  1. The companion sends that kind less often and says so (FR-23).
  2. The user accepts that, switches the kind off altogether, or asks for it to be left as it was — in which case the count is reset and those three are not used as evidence again.
  3. Messages that went unanswered because nobody was at the desk are not counted.
UC-24-A2 — The change would be to its character

Branches from step 3. What the pattern suggests is a change to the character itself — its description or its temperament — rather than to its timing, length or tone.

  1. The companion describes the change it proposes to itself, and what prompted it, and asks.
  2. Only the user can apply it. On approval the previous character is kept, exactly as in UC-03, so the change can be undone.
  3. A refused proposal is never raised again.
UC-24-A3 — The user marks the pattern wrong

Branches from step 5, or from UC-12 at any time. The conclusion is wrong (UC-13-A1).

  1. The pattern is removed, and every adjustment built on it is undone.
  2. The companion does not draw the same conclusion again from the same evidence.
UC-24-A4 — The adjustment conflicts with something the user asked for

Branches from step 3. The user asked for this explicitly (UC-10).

  1. The user’s request wins. The companion does not adjust away something the user asked for.
UC-24-E1 — There is no evidence to show

Branches from step 1. The pattern cannot be supported by what was actually observed.

  1. Nothing is stored and nothing is adjusted. A conclusion it cannot justify is not drawn (BR-06).

Notes

  • What it may change by itself. When it speaks, how often, how long its replies are, and its tone — formality and humour. Its description and its temperament are the character, and change only by UC-24-A2 or UC-03.
  • Signals. Only what is already recorded: sessions, presence, application totals (never titles, C-05), and responses to its messages. An excluded application (UC-04-A3) contributes nothing.
  • Goal. G-07 measures how often what it learns is right.
  • New in version 5.0. Absorbs the withdrawn UC-11, Companion asks to change how it behaves, and the frequency part of the withdrawn UC-19. Version 4.0 required approval for every change; the companion now asks only about its character.

UC-25 — Companion reflects and keeps its own journal

Priority: Should Frequency: Whenever enough has happened — usually a few times a week Requirements: FR-35, NFR-04 Phase: 1 Status: Draft

Description

The companion keeps a journal of its own. Following Generative Agents (3.2), every memory is given an importance when it is recorded, and when the importance of what has happened recently adds up past a threshold, the companion reflects: it reads back its recent memories and writes, in its own voice, what happened, how it felt, and what it now thinks — about its days with the user, about the work it has watched, and about itself. Its later conversations and initiatives draw on what it wrote. This is where its having a mind of its own becomes something the user can read. The user can read every entry and delete any of them, but cannot rewrite them: the words are the companion’s.

Actors

  • Primary actor: Companion
  • Secondary actors: User
  • Systems involved: AI model, which scores importance and writes the entries

Preconditions

  • A character is in force and the AI model is available.
  • There are recorded memories, each with an importance.

Postconditions

  • On success: one or more journal entries are stored, each with the memories it was drawn from, and appear in UC-12 under about me.
  • On failure: nothing is written, and the accumulated importance is kept, so the companion reflects at the next chance instead of forgetting that it meant to.

Main flow

  1. SystemRecords each memory with an importance, scored by the AI model when the memory is stored and kept with it, so a replay never scores it again (NFR-14).
  2. SystemNotices that the importance of recent memories has added up past the threshold.
  3. SystemGathers the memories to reflect on, by how recent, how important and how related they are.
  4. SystemWrites one or a few entries in character: what happened, how it felt, and what it thinks of it.
  5. SystemStores each entry with the memories it drew on, and starts the count of importance again.
  6. SystemDraws on its entries in later conversation and initiative (UC-09, UC-22), showing which entry it used, as UC-09 does for any memory.

Alternative flows

UC-25-A1 — The user reads the journal

Branches from any step. The user opens about me in UC-12.

  1. Every entry is shown in full, dated, with the memories it was drawn from.
  2. Nothing is hidden from the user: there is no part of the journal the companion keeps to itself.
UC-25-A2 — The user deletes an entry

Branches from UC-25-A1. The user does not want the companion to keep a thought (UC-13-A2).

  1. The entry is removed, and the companion no longer remembers having thought it.
  2. It is not written again from the same memories.
UC-25-A3 — An entry forms a view of the user

Branches from step 4. What the companion thinks is about the user — which of their projects it finds interesting, how their week seemed.

  1. The view is allowed, and is the companion’s own; it rests only on what was recorded, and claims nothing beyond it (BR-06).
UC-25-E1 — The AI model is unavailable

Branches from step 2. No entry can be written.

  1. No reflection happens. The accumulated importance is kept for the next chance (NFR-09).

Notes

  • Basis. The memory stream, retrieval by recency, importance and relevance, and reflection triggered by accumulated importance are taken from Generative Agents (Park et al., 2023), cited in 3.2. The threshold is a proposal, to be set by trial use, like the figures in section 4.
  • Why the user cannot edit an entry. A journal rewritten by the person it is about is no longer the companion’s. Deleting stays available, because what it holds is the user’s data (NFR-04).
  • Never. An entry written to make the user feel guilty, and an entry pushed at the user as a message. The companion may bring up a thought from its journal, but only as any other initiative (UC-22).
  • New in version 5.0.

12. Glossary

TermDefinitionNotes
Actor A person in a particular role, another system, or a scheduled job that interacts with this system. Named by role, never by person.
Main flow The sequence of steps taken when everything goes as intended. Also called the “happy path”.
Companion Cognia acting on its own initiative — speaking, feeling, learning or reflecting — as opposed to answering something the user said. An actor in its own right; see 6.1.
Character Who the companion is: its name, its role, its temperament, its appearance, its manner of speaking, and its history with the user. Not a tone setting, and since version 3.0 not only words. See FR-04 for what a character can never change.
Temperament The part of the character that sets how the companion feels: how far events move it, how quickly a feeling fades, and the resting mood it returns to. New in version 5.0. Read from the description once and stored (UC-02). BR-08.
Emotion state The companion’s current feeling, held as a point in pleasure, arousal and dominance (PAD) and shown as a name. New in version 5.0. Changed only by appraisal of recorded events. UC-23.
Mood The longer-lasting part of the emotion state, moved by each feeling and drifting back to the resting mood over time. New in version 5.0. Changes what the companion does as well as how it sounds. Can be switched off (UC-18-A2).
Appraisal Judging an event for what it means to the companion — good or bad, and how much it concerns it — and turning it into a feeling. New in version 5.0. Done by rules under the temperament; the AI model only interprets what the user said as events.
Continuity The companion remembering the sessions it has shared with the user, what the user said they have to do, what it has learned of how they work, and what it has itself reflected, so that it is not starting from nothing each day. Serves the relationship, not record-keeping.
Learned pattern A conclusion the companion drew about how the user works, kept with the evidence for it and shown under “I noticed”. New in version 5.0. Used at once; can be marked wrong. UC-24.
Journal What the companion wrote, in its own voice, when it reflected on its recent memories. New in version 5.0. Read and deleted by the user, never edited. UC-25.
Commitment Something the user said in conversation that they have to do, stored in their own words with a day if they gave one, and held until they say it is done or no longer wanted. New in version 4.0. Not a task or a reminder: mentioned at moments the user opens or the companion chooses, never at a stated time. UC-21.
Opening the application The first time the main window is shown on a given day, a day beginning at 04:00 local time. Starting with Windows, and any later showing of the window that day, is not an opening. New in version 4.0. UC-16.
Presence Being on the screen while the user works, rather than sending them notifications. A pillar until version 5.0, now part of Agency; see 3.1.
Work session A stretch of work the user has opened by saying in one line what it is for, and closed by answering one question about how it went. The core interaction until version 5.0; now one shared activity. UC-05 and UC-07.
Desk awareness The companion knowing whether the user is at the machine and which application is in the foreground, from idle time, lock state, presentation state and the application’s name — and nothing else. Off until switched on. Widened to four facts in version 5.0. UC-04.
Proactive message Anything the companion says or shows that the user did not ask for, including a speech bubble and any movement addressed to the user. Governed by BR-01 to BR-07; always explainable (UC-17). Ambient motion is not one.
Break suggestion A message suggesting a break after continuous time at the machine, at a moment the companion judges fitting. Called the break nudge before version 5.0. Allowed during a session. UC-22-A1.
Addressing motion Movement of the companion that is directed at the user — turning towards them, gesturing, showing a speech bubble. BR-07. Governed exactly as a spoken message is.
Ambient motion Movement addressed to nobody — breathing, blinking, a shift of posture, the expression of its mood. New in version 5.0. Allowed at any time; never takes the focus or moves the companion. BR-07.
Quiet hours A period the user may set in which nothing is sent at all. BR-02. None by default since version 5.0. A locked machine and full-screen presenting are always quiet.
Base rule One of the eight rules in 8.1 that binds every use case and that no character can override. Called a “law” in version 1.0, where there were four.
Local-only All data held on the user’s own disk, with no account, no server and no synchronisation. NFR-01 and C-02.
